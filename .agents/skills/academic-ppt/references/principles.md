# Principles behind the interview and the design doc

## Yanai — story first, explain don't impress

> Source: '/Users/Reid Hu/OrbitOS/50_Resources/SelfDevelopment/Yanai_How_to_Give_a_Good_[Talk.md](http://Talk.md)' (partial capture, Sections 1–3 only).

- Before any slide exists, answer three questions: (1) What is my core conclusion? (2) What background does the audience need to understand it? (3) What can be deleted without hurting the main storyline?
- A talk exists to **explain**, not to **impress**. Success = the audience remembers *what problem, why it matters, what you found* — not how much work was done.
- Every additional slide adds cognitive load. Delete-test each page: "if I cut this, does the main storyline still hold?" If yes → cut.
- Every on-slide element (figure, arrow, label, color, statistical symbol) must be spoken to; anything unspoken is a distraction.
- Slide titles are summarizing sentences, not vague words like "Results".



## Winston — How to Speak (MIT OCW RES.TLL-005, IAP 2018)

Premise: success is determined by ability to speak, ability to write, and quality of ideas — in that order. Quality of a talk = Knowledge × Practice × Talent, in that order of weight: preparation beats charisma.

**Start**

- Open with an **empowerment promise**: exactly what they'll know/be able to do by the end. It's a contract for their attention.
- Never open with a joke — the audience hasn't tuned to you yet. (A joke at the *end* is fine.)

**Hold attention (build into structure)**

- **Cycle**: state each important idea ~3 times from different angles — at any instant ~20% of the room is fogged out.
- **Fence**: explicitly distinguish your idea from neighboring/prior work so it isn't mistaken for something known.
- **Verbal punctuation**: enumerated landmarks ("second of three…") give the momentarily-lost a place to climb back on.
- **Ask a question** (neither trivial nor too hard), then wait ~7 seconds.

**Slides (de-facto design checklist)**

- Few words per slide — people have one language processor; on-slide text competes with your voice.
- Never read slides aloud. Kill small fonts, logos, title/orientation junk, decoration — anything that isn't the idea steals from the idea.
- No laser pointer (turns you away from the room); build slides so pointing is unnecessary.
- Boards inform (pace matches absorption), slides expose (much material, fast). Props are memorable; a bullet isn't.

**Special cases**

- Oral exam / defense: the classic failure is no situation and no problem statement — set stakes before method.
- Job talk: within the first five minutes show a **vision** (problem people care about + your approach) AND that you've **done something**.

**Being remembered — the five S's**

- Attach a **Symbol**, **Slogan**, **Surprise**, **Salient idea** (one thing that sticks out), and **Story** to the work. Ideas get used when they carry a handle.

**Stop**

- Final slide = **Contributions** — the last image persists; don't spend it on "Thank you", "Questions?", collaborators, or a URL.
- Final words: a joke is fine now; salute the audience. Don't end on a bare "thank you".

**Delivery-only (not deck design):** ~11am, well-lit room sized to the crowd, case the room beforehand; eye contact, body to the room.

Sources: MIT OCW course + transcript (ocw.mit.edu/courses/res-tll-005-how-to-speak-january-iap-2018/), corroborated by published lecture notes.

## Alon — premise titles (Molecular Cell 36, 165, 2009)

- Whole talk = one **premise**: a full sentence (subject, verb, object). It decides what gets in; cool but unrelated material goes out.
- Each slide title = that slide's own premise sentence. Avoid question titles and fragments. Two premises → two slides.
- Remember how much the audience does not know; a clear introduction is an act of care.
- Arc (Aristotle's two wonders): wonder at the phenomenon → tension as the gap is described → "so that is what the answer looks like."
- Fold audience questions into the next version of the talk.

## Minto — SCQA opening (The Pyramid Principle)

- **S**ituation (what the audience already accepts) → **C**omplication (what breaks it) → **Q**uestion (the one it raises) → **A**nswer (the talk).
- S + C = the stakes; Q = the first-glance question.

## Parker & Stone / Olson — "but" and "therefore" (NYU 2011; *Houston, We Have a Narrative*, 2015)

- Beats joined by "and then" are a list; beats joined by "but" or "therefore" are a story.
- ABT = And (setup) → But (problem) → Therefore (answer). Works at sentence, slide, and talk scale.

## Synthesis (ours, not any single source)

- Open with SCQA: a stakes page (S + C), then the first-glance question as outline item 1.
- Outline items = the audience's questions, each raised by the previous answer (a but/therefore link). Content slide titles = premise-sentence answers (Alon). Questions live on section dividers, never on content titles.
