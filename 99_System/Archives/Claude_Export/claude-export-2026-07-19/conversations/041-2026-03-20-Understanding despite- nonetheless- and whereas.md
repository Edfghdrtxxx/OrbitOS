You're on the right track with the general idea, but there are some important corrections needed.

**"Despite"** introduces a concession — it means "in spite of." The critical grammar point is that **despite** takes a noun phrase or gerund, never a full clause with a subject + finite verb. So your example needs fixing:

- ✗ *Despite I can't go* (wrong — full clause after "despite")
- ✓ *Despite not being able to go, I appreciate the invitation.*
- ✓ *Despite my inability to go, I appreciate the invitation.*

If you want to keep the clause structure "I can't go," use **although** or **even though** instead: *Although I can't go, I appreciate the invitation.*

**"Nonetheless"** — your example is perfect. It's a conjunctive adverb that links two independent sentences, where the second one holds true *against the expectation* set by the first. So "despite" and "nonetheless" do express a similar logical relationship (concession), just with different grammar: one works *within* a sentence, the other *between* sentences.

**"Whereas"** — this is where things go off course. "Whereas" signals **contrast between two parallel facts**, not concession. It doesn't mean "but on the other hand" as a sentence opener. Grammatically, it's a subordinating conjunction that joins two clauses *within* one sentence:

- ✗ *I can't go. Whereas, my girlfriend can.* (fragment — "whereas" can't start an independent sentence like this)
- ✓ *I can't go, whereas my girlfriend can.*

And notice the meaning shift: "whereas" simply puts two facts side by side for comparison, with no sense of "surprising" or "in spite of." It's closer to "while" or "by contrast."

So to summarize the three relationships: **despite** and **nonetheless** share the same *concessive* logic (X is true, but Y holds anyway), just at different grammatical levels. **Whereas** belongs to a different category entirely — it's about *neutral contrast*, not overcoming an expectation.
