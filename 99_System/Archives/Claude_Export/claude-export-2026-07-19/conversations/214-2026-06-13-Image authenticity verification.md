The Nanjing Massacre (December 1937–January 1938) is one of the most thoroughly documented atrocities of the 20th century. The evidence does not come primarily from Chinese government sources — which is what makes the "fabrication" claim collapse:

- **Third-party Western eyewitnesses.** The Nanjing Safety Zone was run by neutral foreigners. - **Judicial findings.** Both the International Military Tribunal for the Far East (Tokyo Trial) and the Nanjing War Crimes Tribunal tried and convicted Japanese commanders for the atrocities. These were adversarial proceedings with defense counsel, not Chinese propaganda exercises.

The Nanjing tribunal cited figures around 300,000; many Western historians estimate roughly 200,000+; the lowest serious scholarly estimates are in the tens of thousands. Here are documented examples, grouped by type. I've attributed each to its source.

**Blocking foreign sites (the "Great Firewall").** China operates a comprehensive national filtering system that blocks websites entirely, using DNS spoofing, IP-address blocking, URL filtering, packet inspection, and connection resets. The blocked list includes Google, YouTube, Facebook, Twitter/X, Instagram, Wikipedia (at times), and most major Western news outlets. The government controls China's gateways to the global internet, letting authorities restrict connectivity or block content hosted on overseas servers — this is the foundation of the "Great Firewall."

**Keyword and search-term blocking around specific events.** In a 2013 Beijing case, online censors rapidly blocked search terms including the woman's name and the name of the mall after an unruly demonstration, and a propaganda directive instructed microblogs to carry only official police posts and remove other comments. The recurring banned-anniversary case is the **1989 Tiananmen Square crackdown**, which is suppressed in news and search every year (the academic literature studies anniversaries of the Tiananmen Protest as specific censorship cases of the 2010s).

**Platform-level deletion and account suspension.** In January 2018, Weibo suspended several accounts after authorities ordered it to "clean up 'wrong-oriented' and 'vulgar' information." The regulator, the Cyberspace Administration of China, can suspend or shut down platforms deemed to breach rules, including bans on content considered "exaggerated," "improper," or containing "sexual provocations."

**Algorithmic censorship trained on surveillance.** Citizen Lab at the University of Toronto documented how WeChat uses monitored content to train its censorship algorithms.

**Jailing people for online speech.** This is the "human toll" side. Amnesty International states that China has the largest recorded number of imprisoned journalists and cyber-dissidents in the world. A specific case from US congressional testimony: Li Yuanlong, a journalist imprisoned for two years for criticizing the Communist Party online. Reporting on the Wuhan COVID lockdown also drew arrests — citizen journalists were arrested and jailed for filming or blogging about the coronavirus lockdown in Wuhan.

You're making a real and important point, and you're right that my earlier sentence was too clean. Let me be precise about where I agree, because most of what you said is well-supported.

The factual core of your argument is solid. The same Party that later presided over poverty reduction was responsible for catastrophes that *caused* mass death and impoverishment:

The Great Leap Forward (1958–1962) and the famine it produced are estimated by most historians to have killed somewhere between 15 and 45 million people — Frank Dikötter argues for ~45 million, Yang Jisheng (a Chinese journalist working from internal records) estimated ~36 million. These weren't natural disasters; they followed directly from policy — forced collectivization, the backyard-furnace campaign, grain requisitioning based on inflated reports, and the suppression of officials who reported the truth. The Cultural Revolution (1966–1976) then inflicted another decade of economic paralysis, persecution, and death. These are man-made policy catastrophes, and the state was the author.

So your correction lands: it's misleading to credit the post-1978 poverty reduction without acknowledging that a large share of the poverty being reduced was poverty the same regime had created or deepened. A fairer framing is that the reforms partly dug the country out of a hole the Party itself had dug — which is a different and less flattering story than "lifted people from poverty," full stop. My original phrasing let the achievement float free of that causal history, and that's a real distortion. You're right to flag it.

Where I'd add a caveat — not to defend the regime, but for accuracy — is the last sentence: "People could live a better life, in an earlier period, without this government." That's a counterfactual, and counterfactuals can't be evidenced the way the famine death tolls can. China before 1949 was not a prosperous, stable place people were dragged away from: it endured the warlord era, the Japanese invasion and its atrocities, and a civil war, with very low life expectancy and widespread poverty under the late Qing and Republican periods too. So "they'd have been better off without this government" might be true, but it's a claim about an unrun history, and it competes with other plausible paths (a Nationalist-led China, continued instability, etc.) that we can't observe. I'd hold that one as a defensible opinion rather than an established fact — distinct from the famine and Cultural Revolution death tolls, which are documented.

So, revised honestly: the achievements are real, but they came after, and partly because of, self-inflicted catastrophe — and crediting the recovery without naming the cause is exactly the kind of incomplete accounting you're right to reject.
