### 6. assistant — 2026-07-04T07:54:44.529484Z

The most transferable takeaways from Winston's talk, drawn from the primary source (the MIT OCW lecture and his own framing):

**1. Open with a promise, not a joke.** Winston's reasoning is mechanical, not stylistic: when the audience just sat down, they're still disengaging from their phones and laptops, and their "language processor" isn't tuned to you yet. He said humans have only one language processor — if it's busy, they can't absorb a joke or your ideas. So the first minute is for telling them what they'll know by the end that they don't know now (his "empowerment promise").

**2. One focus at a time; strip the slides.** His slide rules follow from the same one-processor premise: wordy slides force people to *read*, which competes with *listening*. He advised removing titles, logos, and unnecessary words, and using few words per slide. If people are reading your slide, they're not hearing you.

**3. Make ideas stick with concreteness and repetition.** Two mechanisms here. First, *cycle on the subject* — go around the idea, come back to it — because at any moment ~20% of the audience is "fogged out," so repetition raises the odds everyone catches it. Second, use imagery and analogy over formulas; presentations with more pictures and less text engage better, and he treated storytelling as central to teaching people how to think.

**4. If your goal is teaching, prefer a board over slides.** A distinction the viral post skipped: Winston said slides are for *exposing* ideas, the board is for *informing/teaching*. The board matches your writing speed to the audience's absorption speed, gives a graphic quality, and gives your hands something to do.

**5. To be *remembered*, use his "Winston Star" (the 5 S's):** Symbol, Slogan, Surprise, Salient idea, and Story. This is for when you want your work to outlast the talk itself.

---

**My own suggestion on what's worth internalizing** (flagged as opinion, not from the source): the single most portable principle is that every rule descends from one constraint — *the audience has one channel for language, and attention is the scarce resource.* If you remember only that, you can regenerate most of the specific rules yourself: don't split their attention, don't make them read and listen at once, and repeat because attention lapses. That's more durable than memorizing the list.

Given your context — you've got interview talks, group presentations, and eventually a thesis defense and PhD-application talks ahead — the promise-opening and the board-vs-slides distinction are probably the two highest-leverage habits to adopt early. Worth watching the full 60-minute lecture once on MIT OpenCourseWare; it's free and he demonstrates each rule live.
