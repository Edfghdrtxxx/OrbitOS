**Now the real problem.** You said you don't know *which* fact to choose. But here's the key insight:

**You don't have to choose. Both facts are given to you. Neither is deleted.**

Look at the correct answer: *"The assistant **who prepares all our samples** **can help**."* — Both "prepares all our samples" AND "can help" are present. Nothing was thrown away. The task was never "pick one fact and discard the other." The task is: **decide which fact goes in which slot.**

So this is not a comprehension problem (choosing meaning). It's a **structural problem** (assigning two known facts to two roles). That reframing removes the anxiety — you already have all the information.

---

**The philosophy — how to assign the two slots:**

Think of a relative-clause sentence as answering **two questions in sequence**:

**Slot 1 — the relative clause — answers "WHICH one?"** (the identity tag)
**Slot 2 — the main clause — answers "What's the POINT?"** (the news)

The trick is to find which fact the reader *already needs* just to know **who/what you're talking about**, versus which fact is the **reason you're saying this sentence at all**.

Let me show you why the test-maker's version is the only sensible one here, using **context**:

> Context: *"The biology lab is missing an important microscope slide."*
> You need someone to fix this problem. You're telling a listener about a person who can solve it.

- The **point** of your sentence — the reason you're speaking — is: **this person CAN HELP.** That's the news your listener wants.
- But *which* person? You identify them by a fact the listener can use to recognize them: **the one who prepares all our samples.**

So: *"The assistant [**who prepares all our samples**] [**can help**]."*
→ "which one?" = prepares our samples. "what's the point?" = can help. ✓

Your version — *"the assistant who can help prepares all our samples"* — says the opposite: it treats "can help" as merely *how to identify* the assistant, and treats "prepares all our samples" as *the news*. But in this context, "prepares all our samples" is background (a routine fact), and "can help" is the urgent point. So your assignment is backwards.

---

**A reliable test you can apply in 5 seconds:**

Ask: **"What is the reader supposed to DO or KNOW after this sentence?"**
That answer is the **main clause**. Everything else is just the identity tag.

Here: the reader should know → *this assistant can help (with your missing-slide problem).* → main clause = "can help." Done.

---

**Why this keeps tripping you:** In many languages (including Chinese), a relative-clause-like modifier and the main predicate don't compete for the same "front" position, so the ordering feels arbitrary. In English, the rule is rigid: **the identity tag clings to the noun; the news goes in the main verb.** Once you internalize "which one? vs. what's the point?", the choice stops being a coin flip.
