**Why Some Animals Glow in the Dark**

Biofluorescence — the ability of an organism to absorb light and re-emit it as a different color — was long thought to be rare. Recent surveys, however, suggest it is widespread among marine creatures, appearing in sharks, sea turtles, and hundreds of fish species. Under blue ocean light, these animals absorb high-energy wavelengths and glow back in greens, reds, and oranges.

Scientists distinguish biofluorescence from bioluminescence, a related but separate phenomenon. Bioluminescent animals, such as fireflies and certain jellyfish, produce their own light through chemical reactions. Fluorescent animals, by contrast, merely transform existing light; in total darkness, they do not glow at all.

The function of biofluorescence remains debated. One hypothesis holds that it aids communication among members of the same species, since many fish can see the fluorescent patterns that are invisible to predators. Another proposes that the glow helps with camouflage, allowing animals to blend into fluorescent coral. A third suggests it plays a role in mating displays.

What makes the trait especially puzzling is its independent evolution across unrelated groups. When a feature arises repeatedly in distant branches of the tree of life, biologists suspect it confers a genuine survival advantage. ## Interview-Style Speaking Question

This mirrors the TOEFL Speaking **Independent Task (Question 1)** format — a personal-opinion question with no reading or listening input.

**Prompt:**

> Some people prefer to plan their daily activities carefully in advance. Others prefer to be spontaneous and decide what to do as the day goes on. Which do you prefer, and why? Use specific reasons and examples to support your answer.

**Format to follow (matches the real test):**
- **Preparation:** 15 seconds
- **Speaking:** 45 seconds

---

### How to run it

1. Set a 15-second prep timer. Jot 2–3 keywords only — don't write full sentences.
2. Record 45 seconds on your phone. State your preference in the first sentence, then give **one** main reason with a specific personal example. Don't try to cram in two reasons; depth beats breadth here.
3. Listen back and self-diagnose against these four points:

| What to check | Ask yourself |
|---|---|
| **Fluency** | Where did I pause, restart, or fill with "uh"/"um"? |
| **Coherence** | Was my structure *opinion → reason → example*, or did I ramble? |
| **Vocabulary/Grammar** | Did I self-correct mid-sentence? Any verb-tense slips? |
| **Completeness** | Did I finish a concluding thought, or did the 45s cut me off? |

Track **where** your fluency broke down, not just whether it did — that granular diagnosis is what actually moves your score.

## Corrected Practice Material — "Take an Interview"

Since the real task gives you **four** questions on one topic, moving from personal experience toward broader opinion, here's a faithful set. No prep time — hit record and start speaking after you read each one.

**Topic: Technology and daily life**

1. How often do you use your phone during a typical day? *(experience/habit)*
2. What is one app or tool you rely on the most, and what do you use it for? *(preference/choice)*
3. Some people think smartphones make us less productive. Do you agree? *(opinion/evaluation)*
4. Do you think people will depend on their phones more or less ten years from now? Why? *(future/policy view)*

### How to run it

For **each** question: read it once, then immediately record **45 seconds** with no notes. Use the Idea → Reason → Tie-in arc above.

After recording all four, listen back and diagnose — tracking *where* fluency broke down, not just whether it did:

| Construct | Self-check question |
