Write it last—but draft a throwaway version early.

The abstract is a compression of the finished paper, so you can't accurately compress something that doesn't exist yet. Your results, framing, and even your main claim tend to shift as you write the body and see the figures come together. If you write the abstract first and treat it as done, you'll almost always end up with a mismatch between what it promises and what the paper delivers. So the final, polished abstract belongs at the very end, after the discussion and conclusion are settled and your numbers are locked.

That said, writing a rough abstract *early*—before or during the first draft—is a genuinely useful exercise, but for a different reason. It forces you to state, in five or six sentences, what your paper is actually about: the gap, what you did, what you found, and why it matters. If you can't write that paragraph cleanly, it's usually a sign the paper's argument isn't clear in your own head yet. Treat that early version as a planning tool and a compass, not a deliverable—expect to throw most of it away.

A practical sequence that works well for an experimental physics paper: figures and results first → methods → introduction → discussion/conclusion → abstract → title. The abstract sits near the end because it draws on nearly every other section.

One caveat on how much of this is fact versus convention: the "write it last" guidance is standard advice across scientific writing guides and journal editors, not an empirical finding—it reflects the structural logic of what an abstract is, so I'd frame it as well-established craft convention rather than something backed by a controlled study. The one hard external constraint is your target journal's abstract rules (word limit, structured vs. unstructured, whether citations are allowed), which you should check against the specific author guidelines before finalizing.
