Great question, Dickim! Despite the name, a neutron star is **not** made purely of neutrons. It does contain protons (and electrons, and potentially more exotic particles deeper inside).

Here's the key physics behind it:

**Why protons must exist — beta equilibrium**

A neutron star settles into a state called **beta equilibrium**, where the forward and reverse weak processes balance each other:

- n → p + e⁻ + ν̄ₑ (neutron decay)
- p + e⁻ → n + νₑ (electron capture)

If you tried to build a star of pure neutrons, the neutron decay channel would immediately start converting some neutrons into protons and electrons until equilibrium is reached. So a small but nonzero proton (and electron) fraction is thermodynamically required.

**Typical composition by layer**

The structure from outside in looks roughly like this:

- **Outer crust**: A lattice of neutron-rich nuclei (like ⁶²Ni, ⁸⁰Zn, etc.) plus a degenerate electron gas — quite similar to white dwarf matter.
- **Inner crust**: Extremely neutron-rich nuclei coexisting with a free neutron gas and electrons. Near the base you get exotic "nuclear pasta" phases.
- **Outer core**: A fluid of mostly neutrons, with roughly 5–10% protons and electrons (plus muons once the electron Fermi energy is high enough). This is the bulk of the star.
- **Inner core** (ρ > 2–3 ρ₀): Still debated — possibilities include hyperons (Σ⁻, Λ, etc.), a kaon or pion condensate, or even deconfined quark matter (u, d, s quarks). Protons are still present in most models.

**Why the proton fraction matters**

The proton fraction x_p is actually astrophysically important: if x_p exceeds roughly 11–15%, the direct Urca process (n → p + e⁻ + ν̄ₑ followed by the reverse) becomes kinematically allowed, which dramatically accelerates neutrino cooling of the star. This is one of the active research frontiers — observed cooling rates of neutron stars constrain the equation of state.

So in short: a pure-neutron star is thermodynamically unstable. Real neutron stars are predominantly neutrons, but the ~5–10% proton-electron component is not a minor detail — it's enforced by weak equilibrium and has major consequences for the star's thermal evolution and stability.
