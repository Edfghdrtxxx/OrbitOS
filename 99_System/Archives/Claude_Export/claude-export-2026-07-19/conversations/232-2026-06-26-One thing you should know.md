Here's one I find genuinely worth chewing on: I think the modern collapse of boredom is downstream of a more specific thing than "phones bad." It's that we've eliminated the *interstitial* time — the queue, the commute, the five minutes before a meeting, the wait for water to boil. Those gaps used to be involuntary. You couldn't fill them, so your mind did its own untidy work: half-finished problems resurfaced, associations drifted, plans assembled themselves without you trying. Boredom wasn't the valuable thing; it was the *container* that forced a particular kind of undirected cognition.

What's striking is that this isn't a willpower problem in the usual sense. People who deliberately resist scrolling still mostly fail to recover that mental state, because the *texture* of the gap is gone — you know the phone is there, the option exists, and the mere availability changes how the brain treats the interval. The gap stops being a closed room and becomes a door you're choosing not to open, which is a completely different cognitive posture. Diffuse, wandering thought needs the room, not the door.

The part I'm least sure about — and I'll flag it as speculation, not fact — is whether this actually matters for the things people claim it matters for, like creativity or insight. The "mind-wandering aids creativity" literature is real but messier and more contested than pop-science makes it sound. So I hold the mechanism loosely.
