### 2. assistant — 2026-06-30T17:10:58.507011Z

These three aren't really near-synonyms — they belong to different word classes and only overlap on the idea of "straight down." Here's the breakdown:

**plumb** — most often an adjective/adverb meaning *exactly vertical* (true to a plumb line). "The wall is plumb." Also a verb: to measure depth or vertical trueness, or figuratively to explore deeply ("plumb the depths"). Note the spelling — no second *m*.

**plummet** — a verb meaning *to fall or drop rapidly* (and a noun for the lead weight on a plumb line). "Stock prices plummeted." You misspelled it as "plumment"; the correct form is **plummet**. The key sense is rapid downward *motion*, not orientation.

**perpendicular** — an adjective/noun from geometry meaning *at a 90° angle to a given line or surface*. It's relational: a line is perpendicular *to* another line. Vertical is just one special case (perpendicular to the horizontal ground).

The core contrasts:

- **plumb** = the *state* of being truly vertical (relative to gravity)
- **plummet** = the *action* of falling fast
- **perpendicular** = a *geometric relationship* of 90°, not inherently vertical

So a falling rock *plummets*; the wall it lands beside is *plumb*; and that wall is *perpendicular* to the floor.

### 4. assistant — 2026-06-30T17:14:43.394441Z

These four are all adverbs, but they do quite different jobs. They cluster loosely around "emphasis," yet each emphasizes a different thing.

**utterly** — an intensifier meaning *completely, totally, to the fullest degree*. It scales up an adjective, usually a negative or extreme one. "utterly exhausted," "utterly ridiculous," "utterly destroyed." It says *how much*, not *how true*. It pairs naturally with absolute or extreme words, not gradable middle-ground ones (you'd say "utterly impossible," rarely "utterly tired-ish").

**literally** — originally means *in the exact, non-figurative sense* — i.e., the words are true word-for-word, not as metaphor. "She literally ran a marathon" (an actual 42 km race, not just "worked hard"). Note: in casual speech it's now widely used as a pure intensifier ("I literally died laughing"), but in academic or formal writing you should keep its strict sense, or you'll sound imprecise. It comments on *the manner of truth* (real vs. figurative).

**definitely** — expresses *certainty* on the speaker's part: *without doubt, for sure*. "I'll definitely be there." It marks how confident you are, not how complete or how literal something is. It answers *how sure?*

**obviously** — means *evidently, in a way clear to everyone*. It claims the truth is *self-evident / easily perceived*. "Obviously, the experiment failed." It comments on how *apparent* something is — and carries a social nuance: it can imply "you should already know this," so it can sound condescending if misused.

The core contrasts, on different axes:

- **utterly** → *degree* (how completely?) — intensifies extent
- **literally** → *mode of truth* (real vs. figurative?)
- **definitely** → *speaker certainty* (how sure am I?)
- **obviously** → *evidentness* (how clear is it to everyone?)

A quick test sentence shows they aren't interchangeable: "He was *utterly* wrong" (completely), "He was *literally* wrong" (wrong in the exact sense — odd here), "He was *definitely* wrong" (I'm certain), "He was *obviously* wrong" (anyone can see it).
