### 2. assistant — 2026-06-25T03:06:55.793814Z

Yes, both exist, but they behave differently.

"It demands that" is a real and common collocation in academic English, especially with a dummy/anticipatory *it* or with *it* referring back to a concept. It takes a that-clause and triggers the subjunctive (mandative subjunctive): "The theory demands that the function *be* continuous," "This interpretation demands that we *reconsider* the assumption." Here *demand* means "require as a necessary condition," which is the dominant sense in academic writing.

"The one demands..." is not a fixed collocation. It would only appear if "the one" is a genuine referring noun phrase pointing to a previously mentioned item (e.g., "Of the two models, the one [that assumes symmetry] demands fewer parameters"). On its own it has no idiomatic status and reads oddly without that antecedent.

A few register notes. In academic prose, *demand* is slightly forceful; *require* is the more neutral and far more frequent choice for "necessitate." So "the proof requires that..." is more common than "the proof demands that..." though both are correct. *Demand* carries a faint connotation of urgency or insistence, which is why it's often used rhetorically: "These results demand explanation," "The data demand a more careful treatment."

If you want the subjunctive pattern, note the verb stays in base form regardless of subject and tense: "demands that it *be*," not "demands that it *is*" (the indicative is acceptable in British usage but the subjunctive is safer in formal writing).

### 4. assistant — 2026-06-25T03:12:17.619465Z

## Other patterns with "demand" (verb)

**demand + noun (direct object)** — to require or call for something
"The task demands precision." "This problem demands attention." Very common in academic prose.

**demand + to-infinitive** — to insist on doing something (the subject does the action)
"She demanded to see the data." Note: this means the demander wants *to do* the thing themselves. Less common in formal academic writing; more interpersonal.

**demand + that-clause (subjunctive)** — the pattern you already saw
"The editor demanded that the figures be revised."

**demand + noun + of/from someone** — to require something from a source
"The method demands a great deal of computational power." "This demands much of the reader." The *of/from* phrase names who/what bears the cost.

Note one gap: *demand* does **not** normally take an object + to-infinitive. English does not allow "demand someone to do X" — that's a frequent error. Use *require* instead ("require someone to do X"), or recast with a that-clause ("demand that someone do X").

## "demand" as a noun

Two senses. The count noun = a forceful request ("their demands were rejected"). The mass noun = the need or desire for something, especially in economics ("demand for housing," "supply and demand"). Common collocations: *meet a demand, in high demand, place demands on, growing demand.*

## "demanding" (adjective)

It means **requiring a lot of effort, skill, attention, or resources** — i.e., hard to satisfy or cope with.

- Of a task/job: "a demanding project" = one that takes great effort.
- Of a person: "a demanding boss" = one who expects a lot and is hard to please.
- Common pairing: *physically/emotionally/intellectually demanding,* *time-demanding* (rare; usually *time-consuming*).

So "demanding" describes the thing (or person) that *places* heavy requirements, while the people on the receiving end are the ones who must meet them. A useful contrast: a task is **demanding**; a person who keeps making demands is also **demanding** — both senses connect back to "calls for a lot."
