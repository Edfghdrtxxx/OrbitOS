Hey Dickim! Great etymology question.

The root you're spotting is actually **vert-/vers-**, from the Latin verb *vertere*, meaning **"to turn."** The spelling shifts between *-vert* and *-vers-* depending on whether the word comes from the infinitive (*vertere*) or the past participle (*versus*, literally "having been turned").

Here's how "turning" plays out in each of your examples:

**-vert forms** (from *vertere*, active "to turn"):
- **invert** → turn inward/upside down
- **revert** → turn back
- **convert** → turn together / turn completely (i.e., transform)

**-vers- forms** (from *versus*, the past participle):
- **inverse** → turned inward/upside down (the state of being inverted)
- **reverse** → turned back
- **converse** → turned together / turned around (facing each other → talking)
- **adverse** → turned against

So every one of these words is just a different Latin prefix glued onto the same core idea of "turning": *in-* (into/against), *re-* (back), *con-* (together/completely), *ad-* (toward/against). The prefix sets the direction; *vert/vers* supplies the motion.

You'll see the same root hiding in less obvious places too — *universe* (turned into one), *versatile* (able to turn easily), *vertigo* (a spinning sensation), even *prose* (from *prosa*, shortened from *proversus* — "turned forward," i.e., straightforward speech, as opposed to verse which "turns" back at the end of each line).
