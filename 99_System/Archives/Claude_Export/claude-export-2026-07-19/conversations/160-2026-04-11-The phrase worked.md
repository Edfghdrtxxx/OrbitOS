The phrase worked because it did something socially rare: **it named his own vulnerability instead of performing confidence**.

When a stranger approaches a group of women at night, the default threat model activates — is this person going to be pushy, weird, aggressive? The social tension spikes.

But "I'm feeling a little overstimulated" short-circuits that entirely, for a few reasons:

1. **It signals self-awareness.** Someone with bad intentions doesn't stop to narrate their inner state. It reads as safe almost immediately.

2. **It's disarming through honesty.** He's admitting weakness in a context where men typically perform strength. That asymmetry is unexpected — and unexpected ≠ threatening.

3. **It shifts the frame.** Instead of *him* approaching *them*, suddenly it's just a person having a human moment. They become witnesses to his experience rather than targets of his attention.

4. **It gives them control.** By being openly non-threatening, he implicitly hands them the power dynamic. They can choose to engage or not, with zero pressure.

The reason it feels emotionally striking — hence 320K likes — is that most people intuitively know social interactions *could* work this way, but almost nobody actually does it. Vulnerability as a social tool is theoretically understood but rarely deployed in the wild.
