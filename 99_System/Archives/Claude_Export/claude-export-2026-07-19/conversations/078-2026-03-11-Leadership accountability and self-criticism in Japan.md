This is an interesting cross-cultural question. The short answer is: **a similar but distinctly different phenomenon exists in Japan**, and it's worth comparing with the Chinese equivalent you may be more familiar with.

---

**In China**, the practice you're describing is well-known — the **检讨书 (jiǎntǎo shū)**, a formal written self-criticism. It is deeply embedded in organizational culture (corporate, military, academic), and managers are commonly expected to submit one when subordinates are injured, make serious mistakes, or when targets are missed. It has roots in Maoist political practice.

---

**In Japan**, there are analogous but culturally distinct practices:

- **始末書 (shimatsusho)** — A formal written explanation and apology submitted by an employee (or manager) after an incident, mistake, or rule violation. It is the closest Japanese equivalent to a self-criticism document. It typically includes an account of what happened, acknowledgment of fault, and a pledge to prevent recurrence.
- **反省 (hansei)** — Deep self-reflection is a core value in Japanese culture and business (famously central to Toyota's production system). Leaders are culturally expected to demonstrate hansei openly after failures.
- **Public bowing and apology press conferences** — Senior executives often hold formal apology press conferences (*owabi kaiken*) after serious workplace accidents, which can include deep, prolonged bows. This is a form of public accountability.
- **Legal accountability** — Under Japan's **Industrial Safety and Health Act (労働安全衛生法)**, managers and employers bear real legal liability for workplace injuries, which reinforces the culture of managerial responsibility.

---

**Key difference**: In China, the self-criticism culture has a stronger ideological and hierarchical-submission dimension (writing it *to* a superior as an act of deference). In Japan, the shimatsusho and public apology culture is more rooted in concepts of **social harmony, shame (恥, *haji*), and collective responsibility**, and tends to be more outward-facing (toward society or the organization) rather than upward-facing (toward a political superior).

So yes — leaders being held accountable for employee injuries and producing formal written acknowledgments of responsibility is very much a real phenomenon in Japan, just expressed through a different cultural lens.
