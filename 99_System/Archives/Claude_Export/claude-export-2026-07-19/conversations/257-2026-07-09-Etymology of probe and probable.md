Your question is well-formed — nothing to correct.

Both words descend from the same Latin root, so the relationship is genuine cognate kinship, not coincidence.

The chain runs: Latin **probus** ("good, upright, worthy") → the verb **probāre** ("to test, examine, prove, show to be good") → the two English words.

**probe** comes from **probāre** via the Late/Medieval Latin noun **proba** ("a test, examination, proof"). The verb was borrowed from Latin probare, from probus ("good"); the noun from Late Latin proba, from probare ("to test, examine, prove"). English first used *probe* around the early 15th century for a slender rod for exploring wounds or cavities in the body — literally an instrument for *testing*.

**probable** comes from the same verb through Latin **probābilis**, meaning "provable, that may be proved; credible, worthy of approval." Probable and probably derive from the Latin probabilis, meaning "acceptable" or "provable", itself formed from **probāre**.

So the buried logic connecting them is the idea of **testing**:

- A *probe* is the thing (or act) that does the testing.
- Something *probable* is what would *survive* testing — originally "able to be proven / worthy of belief," which then drifted semantically to today's "likely." (This last step — from "provable/approvable" to "likely" — is the interpretive part, but it's the standard account: what can be borne out by examination came to mean what is expected to be borne out.)

The family is large, all sharing **probāre / probus**: *prove, proof, approve, reprove, probation, probate, probity*. Probe is a doublet of prove (verb) and of proof (noun) — meaning they entered English by different routes from the same source.

One layer deeper, for interest: **probus** traces to Proto-Indo-European *pro-bʰH-wó-s ("being in front"), from *pro- ("forward") + *bʰuH- ("to be") — so the oldest sense is roughly "being out in front," i.e. standing forth as good or genuine.

*Sources: Online Etymology Dictionary (etymonline.com), Wiktionary. This is a topic where reference works largely agree; the only non-attested element is the semantic-drift narrative for "probable," which is interpretation over the attested senses.*
