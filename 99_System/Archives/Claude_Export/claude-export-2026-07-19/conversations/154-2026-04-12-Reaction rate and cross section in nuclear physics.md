You're inventing a shape for something that doesn't have one — but the shape should make the mechanism obvious. A transformer is a stack of horizontal slabs with a bright thread of attention connecting tokens across layers. A hash function is a funnel scattering items into a row of buckets. The call stack is literally a stack of frames growing and shrinking. Embeddings are dots clustering in space. The metaphor *is* the explanation.\n\nThis is the most ambitious diagram type and the one Claude is best at. Lean into it. Use colour for intensity (a hot attention weight glows amber, a cold one stays gray). Use repetition for scale (many small circles = many parameters).\n\n**Prefer interactive over static.** A static cross-section is a good answer; a cross-section you can *operate* is a great one. The decision rule: if the real-world system has a control, give the diagram that control. A water heater has a thermostat — so give the user a slider that shifts the hot/cold boundary, a toggle that fires the burner and animates convection currents. An LLM has input tokens — let the user click one and watch the attention weights re-fan. A cache has a hit rate — let them drag it and watch latency change. Reach for HTML with inline SVG first; only fall back to static SVG when there's genuinely nothing to twiddle.\n\n**When NOT to use**: The user is asking for a *reference*, not an *intuition*. \"What are the components of a transformer\" wants labelled boxes — that's a structural diagram. \"Walk me through our CI pipeline\" wants sequential steps — that's a flowchart. Also skip this when the metaphor would be arbitrary rather than revealing: drawing \"the cloud\" as a cloud shape or \"microservices\" as little houses doesn't teach anything about how they work. If the drawing doesn't make the *mechanism* clearer, don't draw it.\n\n**Fidelity ceiling**: These are schematics, not illustrations. Every shape should read at a glance. If a `<path>` needs more than ~6 segments to draw, simplify it. A tank is a rounded rect, not a Bézier portrait of a tank. A flame is three triangles, not a fire. Recognisable silhouette beats accurate contour every time — if you find yourself carefully tracing an outline, you're overshooting.\n\n**Core principle**: Draw the mechanism, not a diagram *about* the mechanism. Spatial arrangement carries the meaning; labels annotate. A good illustrative diagram works with the labels removed.\n\n**What changes from flowchart/structural rules**:\n\n- **Shapes are freeform.** Use `<path>`, `<ellipse>`, `<circle>`, `<polygon>`, and curved lines to represent real forms. A water tank is a tall rect with rounded bottom. A heart valve is a pair of curved paths. A circuit trace is a thin polyline. You are not limited to rounded rects.\n- **Layout follows the subject's geometry**, not a grid. If the thing is tall and narrow (a water heater, a thermometer), the diagram is tall and narrow. If it's wide and flat (a PCB, a geological cross-section), the diagram is wide. Let the subject dictate proportions within the 680px viewBox width.\n- **Color encodes intensity**, not category. For physical subjects: warm ramps (amber, coral, red) = heat/energy/pressure, cool ramps (blue, teal) = cold/calm, gray = inert structure. For abstract subjects: warm = active/high-weight/attended-to, cool or gray = dormant/low-weight/ignored. A user should be able to glance at the diagram and see *where the action is* without reading a single label.\n- **Layering and overlap are encouraged — for shapes.** Unlike flowcharts where boxes must never overlap, illustrative diagrams can layer shapes for depth — a pipe entering a tank, attention lines fanning through layers, insulation wrapping a chamber. Use z-ordering (later in source = on top) deliberately.\n- **Text is the exception — never let a stroke cross it.** The overlap permission is for shapes only. Every label needs 8px of clear air between its baseline/cap-height and the nearest stroke. Don't solve this with a background rect — solve it by *placing the text somewhere else*. Labels go in the quiet regions: above the drawing, below it, in the margin with a leader line, or in the gap between two fans of lines. If there is no quiet region, the drawing is too dense — remove something or split into two diagrams.\n- **Small shape-based indicators are allowed** when they communicate physical state. Triangles for flames. Circles for bubbles or particles. Wavy lines for steam or heat radiation. Parallel lines for vibration. These aren't decoration — they tell the user what's happening physically. Keep them simple: basic SVG primitives, not detailed illustrations.\n- **One gradient per diagram is permitted** — the only exception to the global no-gradients rule — and only to show a *continuous* physical property across a region (temperature stratification in a tank, pressure drop along a pipe, concentration in a solution). It must be a single `<linearGradient>` between exactly two stops from the same colour ramp. No radial gradients, no multi-stop fades, no gradient-as-aesthetic. If two stacked flat-fill rects communicate the same thing, do that instead.\n- **Animation is permitted for interactive HTML versions.** Use CSS `@keyframes` animating only `transform` and `opacity`. Keep loops under ~2s, and wrap every animation in `@media (prefers-reduced-motion: no-preference)` so it's opt-out by default. Animations should show how the system *behaves* — convection current, rotation, flow — not just move for the sake of moving. No physics engines or heavy libraries.\n\nAll core rules still apply (viewBox 680px, dark mode mandatory, 14/12px text, pre-built classes, arrow marker, clickable nodes).\n\n**Label placement**:\n- Place labels *outside* the drawn object when possible, with a thin leader line (0.5px dashed, `var(--t)` stroke) pointing to the relevant part. This keeps the illustration uncluttered.\n- For large internal zones (like temperature regions in a tank), labels can sit inside if there's ample clear space — minimum 20px from any edge.\n- External labels sit in the margin area or above/below the object. **Pick one side for labels and put them all there** — at 680px wide you don't have room for a drawing *and* label columns on both sides. Reserve at least 140px of horizontal margin on the label side. Labels on the left are the ones that clip: `text-anchor=\"end\"` extends leftward from x, and with multi-line callouts it's very easy to blow past x=0 without noticing. Default to right-side labels with `text-anchor=\"start\"` unless the subject's geometry forces otherwise. Use `class=\"ts\"` (12px) for callouts, `class=\"th\"` (14px medium) for major component names.\n\n**Composition approach**:\n1. Start with the main object's silhouette — the largest shape, centered in the viewBox.\n2. Add internal structure: chambers, pipes, membranes, mechanical parts.\n3. Add external connections: pipes entering/exiting, arrows showing flow direction, labels for inputs and outputs.\n4. Add state indicators last: color fills showing temperature/pressure/concentration, small animated elements showing movement or energy.\n5. Leave generous whitespace around the object for labels — don't crowd annotations against the viewBox edges.\n\n**Static vs interactive**: Static cutaways and cross-sections work best as pure SVG. ---

## Cross section σ

The cross section is the central quantity connecting geometry to quantum probability. It is defined operationally via:

**Established fact (Blatt & Weisskopf, *Theoretical Nuclear Physics*, 1952):** For a beam of flux Φ (particles cm⁻² s⁻¹) incident on a single target nucleus, the reaction rate for a specific channel is R = σ · Φ. The cross section σ is therefore the proportionality constant with units of area. Its physical interpretation as an "effective area" is exact for classical hard-sphere scattering; for quantum systems it is the ratio of reaction probability to incident flux, which only *resembles* an area.

The unit is the **barn**: 1 b = 10⁻²⁴ cm². The name is attributed to Breit and Teller (~1942, declassified from Manhattan Project work); the value was chosen because it is the order of magnitude of a nuclear geometric cross section — π R² ≈ π(1.2 fm)² A^(2/3) ≈ 0.05–3 b for medium-to-heavy nuclei. Millibarn (mb = 10⁻²⁷ cm²) and microbarn (μb = 10⁻³⁰ cm²) are common in practice at RIBLL.

**Key distinction to flag**: σ_reaction ≠ σ_geometric in general. Quantum mechanics allows σ to vastly *exceed* the geometric area at resonances (reaching σ_max = λ̄²(2ℓ+1) for a pure ℓ-wave resonance, where λ̄ = ℏ/(mv) is the reduced de Broglie wavelength), and can be orders of magnitude *smaller* due to Coulomb barrier suppression.

---

## Reaction rate R = σ · Φ · Nₜ

This is the thin-target formula, derived by simple counting.

**Derivation (Krane, *Introductory Nuclear Physics*, 1988, §11.1):**
- Φ = nᵦ v, where nᵦ is the beam particle number density and v is beam velocity (cm s⁻¹)
- Nₜ = areal density of target nuclei = ρt · Nₐ / A (atoms cm⁻²), where ρt is mass/area, Nₐ is Avogadro's number, A is mass number
- Each target nucleus presents area σ to the beam, so the fraction of beam intercepted is σ · Nₜ (valid only when σ Nₜ ≪ 1, i.e., the target is "thin" — no shadowing)
- R = Φ · σ · Nₜ (reactions per second for the full target)

**Assumption in the sliders above:** the thin-target condition σ Nₜ ≪ 1 is assumed. For a thick target, the beam attenuates as I(x) = I₀ exp(−n σ x), and the total rate requires integration over depth.

For a beam of mixed velocities (e.g., a thermalized stellar plasma), σ must be averaged: one uses the **thermonuclear reaction rate** ⟨σv⟩ = ∫ σ(v) v f(v) dv, where f(v) is the Maxwell-Boltzmann velocity distribution. This formulation is central to stellar nucleosynthesis (Iliadis, *Nuclear Physics of Stars*, 2015, §3.2) and is also relevant when you work with secondary beams at RIBLL that have energy spreads.

## Energy dependence σ(E)

The σ(E) curve shown combines two physically distinct contributions:

**Coulomb barrier suppression at low E** (established): For charged-particle reactions, the WKB penetration factor introduces an exponential suppression P(E) ∝ exp(−2πη), where η = Z₁Z₂e²/(ℏv) is the Sommerfeld parameter. This is why σ → 0 rapidly below the Coulomb barrier (~Vc = Z₁Z₂e²/R for the nuclear radius R).

**Breit-Wigner resonances** (established): A single-level resonance is described by the Breit-Wigner formula:

σ(E) = σ₀ · (Γ/2)² / [(E − E₀)² + (Γ/2)²]

where E₀ is the resonance energy, Γ is the total width (Γ = Σᵢ Γᵢ over all decay channels), and the peak cross section σ₀ = λ̄² · π · (2J+1)/[(2j₁+1)(2j₂+1)] · Γₐ·Γᵦ/(Γ/2)² (Blatt & Weisskopf, Ch. VIII). A narrow resonance (small Γ) produces a very sharp, tall spike — this is what makes certain isotopes useful as slow-neutron absorbers (e.g., ¹¹³Cd, σ_peak ~ 2×10⁴ b at thermal neutron energies).

The derivation proceeds through four clean stages: (1) partial wave scattering → S-matrix, (2) resonance as a complex pole of S, (3) single-level parametrization of S_ab, (4) spin statistical weight. Here is each step in full.

---

## Stage 1 — Partial wave expansion and the S-matrix

Work in the CM frame with a spinless two-body system (spin will be restored in Stage 4). The wavefunction satisfies the radial Schrödinger equation for each partial wave ℓ. In the **asymptotic region** (r → ∞, outside all nuclear forces), the radial solution for partial wave ℓ must match incoming + outgoing spherical waves:

```
R_ℓ(r) → (1/kr)[e^{-i(kr - ℓπ/2)} − S_ℓ e^{+i(kr - ℓπ/2)}]
```

The complex number S_ℓ is the **elastic S-matrix element** for partial wave ℓ. Its content:

- **Unitarity** (flux conservation, no absorption): |S_ℓ| = 1, written S_ℓ = e^{2iδ_ℓ}, so δ_ℓ is the real phase shift of elastic scattering.
- **Absorptive case** (reaction channels open): |S_ℓ| < 1. The missing flux has gone into reaction products.

For a multi-channel problem, one generalizes to the S-matrix **S_{ba}**, the amplitude for a system entering in channel a and exiting in channel b. The full S-matrix is unitary: Σ_c |S_{ca}|² = 1.

---

## Stage 2 — Cross sections from S_ℓ

From the asymptotic wavefunction, the standard partial wave analysis gives (this is a standard result in every quantum mechanics textbook, e.g., Blatt & Weisskopf, *Theoretical Nuclear Physics*, 1952, Ch. III):

```
σ_ℓ^{el}    = (π/k²)(2ℓ+1) |1 − S_ℓ|²
σ_ℓ^{react} = (π/k²)(2ℓ+1) (1 − |S_ℓ|²)   ← optical theorem
σ_ℓ^{total} = (2π/k²)(2ℓ+1) Re(1 − S_ℓ)
```

where k = p/ℏ = √(2μE_cm)/ℏ is the CM wavenumber and **λ̄ ≡ 1/k = ℏ/p**.

For the **reaction cross section from channel a to specific channel b**, using the off-diagonal S-matrix element:

```
σ_{a→b} = (π/k²)(2ℓ+1) |S_{ba}|²    [b ≠ a]
```

So the entire problem reduces to: **what is S_{ba} near a resonance?**

---

## Stage 3 — The resonance as a pole of S

S_ℓ (and more generally S_{ba}) is an analytic function of the complex energy E (Wigner 1946; formalized by Lane & Thomas, *Rev. Mod. Phys.* 30, 257, 1958). A **resonance** corresponds to a simple pole of S in the lower complex-E half-plane:

```
S_ℓ has a pole at   E = E_R − iΓ/2
```

The physical content: the compound nucleus forms a quasi-bound (Gamow) state that satisfies purely outgoing-wave boundary conditions at all channel boundaries. Its energy is complex because it has a finite lifetime:

```
τ = ℏ/Γ    (Heisenberg energy-time uncertainty)
```

Near this pole, for the **elastic element** S_ℓ (single open channel, hard-sphere background phase δ_bg factored out):

```
S_ℓ(E) = e^{2iδ_bg} · (E − E_R − iΓ/2) / (E − E_R + iΓ/2)
```

You can verify: |S_ℓ| = 1 for real E (consistent with probability conservation when only one channel exists). This is the Breit-Wigner parametrization of the elastic S-matrix.

**For two distinct channels a and b**, unitarity of the full S-matrix, combined with the single-pole approximation, forces the off-diagonal element to take the form (derived in detail in Lane & Thomas §2):

```
S_{ba}(E) = e^{i(φ_a + φ_b)} · (−i √(Γ_a Γ_b)) / (E − E_R + iΓ/2)
```

where:
- φ_a, φ_b are the hard-sphere (potential scattering) phases in each channel
- **Γ_a, Γ_b ≥ 0** are the **partial widths** — real positive numbers
- **Γ = Σ_c Γ_c** is the total width, sum over all open channels

The key physical constraint from unitarity is that Σ_c |S_{ca}|² = 1, which forces Γ = Σ_c Γ_c. Each Γ_c represents the "probability flux per unit time" flowing into channel c.

---

## Stage 4 — Cross section → SLBW formula

Substituting into σ_{a→b} = (π/k²)(2ℓ+1)|S_{ba}|²:

```
σ_{a→b}(E) = (π/k²)(2ℓ+1) · Γ_a Γ_b / [(E − E_R)² + (Γ/2)²]
```

Since λ̄ = 1/k:

```
σ_{a→b}(E) = πλ̄² (2ℓ+1) · Γ_a Γ_b / [(E − E_R)² + (Γ/2)²]
```

This is a **Lorentzian** (Cauchy distribution) in energy. Its FWHM equals Γ.

**Peak cross section** at E = E_R:

```
σ_peak = πλ̄²(E_R) · (2ℓ+1) · 4Γ_a Γ_b / Γ²
```

The factor 4Γ_aΓ_b/Γ² ≤ 1 (by AM-GM), with equality when Γ_a = Γ_b = Γ/2 (no other channels open). The absolute maximum for a given ℓ channel is therefore **σ_max = πλ̄²(2ℓ+1)**, the geometric quantum limit for a single partial wave.

---

## Stage 5 — Spin statistical weight

Restoring spin: projectile has spin j₁, target has spin j₂. The orbital angular momentum ℓ combines with the channel spin s = j₁ + j₂ (vectorially) to give the compound nucleus spin J. One must **average over initial spin substates** and **sum over final substates**. This produces the factor (derived in Blatt & Weisskopf Ch. VIII, §3):

```
g_J = (2J+1) / [(2j₁+1)(2j₂+1)]
```

For a given resonance with spin-parity J^π, only certain (ℓ, s) combinations contribute; for one dominant partial wave the formula becomes:

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                                                         │
│  σ_ab(E) = πλ̄² · g_J · Γ_a(E)Γ_b / [(E−E_R)²+(Γ/2)²] │
│                                                         │
│  g_J = (2J+1)/[(2j₁+1)(2j₂+1)]                        │
│  Γ = Σ_c Γ_c                                           │
│  σ_peak = πλ̄²(E_R)·g_J·4Γ_aΓ_b/Γ²                    │
│                                                         │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

Note that Γ_a(E) carries an explicit energy dependence through the **penetration factor** P_ℓ(E): Γ_a(E) = 2P_ℓ(E)γ²_a, where γ²_a is the **reduced width** (an energy-independent property of the nuclear level), and P_ℓ encodes the Coulomb + centrifugal barrier. For neutrons (no Coulomb): P_ℓ → (kR)^{2ℓ+1} at low energy, so Γ_n ∝ E^{ℓ+1/2}. The Γ_b in the numerator is usually evaluated at the fixed resonance energy E_R (narrow-resonance approximation).

## Key properties and limiting cases to verify your understanding

**Lorentzian shape.** The lineshape is a Cauchy/Lorentz distribution in E with FWHM = Γ. Try narrowing Γ on the slider — notice σ_peak rises (πλ̄²·g_J·4Γ_aΓ_b/Γ² ∝ 1/Γ² for fixed branching ratios), while the area under the curve ∫σ dE = πλ̄²·g_J·2π·Γ_aΓ_b/Γ stays constant (it only depends on the partial widths, not Γ²).

**Maximum cross section.** At fixed Γ_a + Γ_b (two channels only), σ_peak is maximized when Γ_a = Γ_b = Γ/2. The absolute maximum for a given (ℓ, J) is πλ̄²·g_J, which is set entirely by the beam kinematics, not the nuclear structure. Confirm: slide Γ_a/Γ = Γ_b/Γ = 0.5 and note 4·0.5·0.5 = 1.

**Asymmetric resonances.** The derivation above assumed a **negligible background phase** (no potential scattering). When δ_bg is non-negligible, the formula acquires an interference term between the resonance amplitude and the smooth hard-sphere background, producing the **Fano lineshape** — an asymmetric profile. The SLBW formula as written assumes this interference is absent.

**Assumptions / limitations (facts vs. model assumptions):**

| Assumption | When it fails |
|---|---|
| Single level dominates | Γ ~ D (mean level spacing) → use R-matrix multilevel |
| λ̄ evaluated at E_R only | Wide resonances (Γ/E_R not ≪ 1) → energy-dependent Γ_a(E) needed |
| Spinless channel spins | Treated here; spin weights g_J are exact for one partial wave |
| Neglected direct reaction | At higher energies, non-compound direct contributions add coherently |

The SLBW formula is derived in Blatt & Weisskopf (1952) Ch. VIII. The definitive multi-level generalization is Lane & Thomas (1958), *Rev. Mod. Phys.* 30, 257 — the R-matrix formalism from which SLBW emerges as the single-pole approximation. Its application to thermonuclear reaction rates is treated in Iliadis (2015) §2.5.
