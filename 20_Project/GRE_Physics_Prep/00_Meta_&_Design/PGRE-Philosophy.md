# Physics GRE Item Philosophy

This document is the whole briefing. An agent that will write practice drills should be able to read it and nothing else, tell a GRE Physics item from a textbook problem, and generate the former. It is a way of thinking about what an item is *for*, not a catalog of topics or templates. Do not treat the moves named below as a menu. Treat them as evidence of a single craft.

## What the test is measuring

ETS’s own sentence is the starting point: the aim of the test is “to determine the extent of the examinees’ grasp of fundamental principles and their ability to apply these principles in the solution of problems.” Most questions, they add, can be answered from mastery of the first three years of undergraduate physics. The *Guide to the Use of Scores* leans a little further: there is increased emphasis on understanding fundamental theoretical principles. Those two claims sit together. The instrument is not a research-aptitude exam, not a qualifier, and not a survey of every elective. It is a speeded, five-option check of whether a typical undergraduate physics major still holds the core and can apply it.

John Conway, after six years on the committee of examiners, is the only first-person public account of the craft from inside the room. Items are short, “to-the-point,” and “take little time once you see what to do.” Writing them is hard; after about six levels of review the committee still finds issues of clarity, reasoning, and even basic physics correctness. Each form arrives at items that “test mastery of a clear physics concept or idea,” with “a certain amount of memorization required in terms of the basic equations learned in undergraduate physics.” Many items can be done using just concepts; many can be done with simple dimensional analysis. When numerical answers appear, “the numbers are chosen so as to allow easy arithmetic — no calculators are allowed.” An item that requires a calculator is a failed item.

The current sitting, in force since September 2023, is computer-delivered: approximately seventy five-choice questions in two hours, no separately timed sections, raw score equal to the number correct. Nothing is subtracted for a wrong answer. Older paper forms were approximately one hundred items in 170 minutes and used formula scoring. Time per item was conserved at about a hundred seconds. ETS described the cut as access and convenience, not as a change in what the test measures. The current official practice booklet is not a new generation of item writing. In the recent released pair it is a deletion-subset of a prior hundred-item form: same stems, same choices, same figures, new numbering. The deletions that made the current booklet were mixed in kind, not a hardness cut. What changed is operational — how many you sit — not how an item is built.

The committee of examiners sets topic percentages from a survey of undergraduate curricula. Classical mechanics and electromagnetism together are about two fifths of the paper; quantum mechanics and atomic physics together about a quarter; thermodynamics, optics and waves, special relativity, laboratory methods, and specialized topics fill the rest in stable smaller slices. A generated set of any real length should not be a mechanics-only quiz. That is a coverage constraint, not a writing method. Do not unfold the specification into chapters. The method of an item is the same whether the costume is a pendulum or a quark table.

Robert Brown’s classroom diagnostic is the inverse of Conway’s production note. Students “do amazingly well on the free-form problems but just bomb the GRE questions,” because courses no longer require them to remember anything, because free-form work offers partial credit, and because they cannot get numbers right without a calculator. He still calls the items “beautiful little problems.” The form is the point. Homework rewards a correct multi-step path with partial credit. This exam rewards a single letter, in about a hundred seconds, with no calculator, no partial credit, and four distractors written by physicists who know which mistakes a hurried undergraduate will make.

## An item is a five-way decision

A GRE Physics item is not a request to produce a result. It is a five-way decision about a physical situation. The stem stages a world just completely enough that one identity applies, then asks which of five accounts of that world is right. The examinee is not being asked to “work the problem” in the textbook sense — set up, derive, substitute, box an answer. They are being asked to *decide*, under a clock, which description survives contact with one governing idea.

That difference from an end-of-chapter exercise is the whole design. A textbook problem is well-posed for computation: every given is needed, the unknown is named, and the path is a derivation whose length is part of the pedagogy. A GRE stem is well-posed for *discrimination*. It may include quantities that never enter, omit the name of the law, ask a qualitative question of a quantitative setup, or ask a quantitative question that a qualitative filter already settles. Computing the answer is one legitimate route. Surviving the filter is another. The form is built so both routes exist.

The paper is a mixture of decisions of unequal cost, not a sequence of equally effortful calculations. Published percent-correct values on released forms run from the teens to the mid-eighties. That mix is a design choice. It lets ETS equate forms, and it makes “skip anything that will eat the clock” a rational first pass. Easy recognition items and items that can consume several minutes if taken as homework sit on the same paper and are worth the same point. That equality is the opposite of a qualifier or a homework set, where a hard problem is worth more.

A large minority of items never want a number. They want an ordering, a direction, a graph, a yes or no on simultaneous measurability, a statement about what is independent of a named parameter, a “which is true,” a “which is NOT true.” That work is first-class, not a warmup. A generated paper that is all calculation is as off-style as a paper that is all slogans.

## What the stem does

The stem’s job is to load a situation and withhold the obvious instruction. It says what is present, what is to be neglected, and what is asked, and it is careful about all three.

It is generous with setup and stingy with procedure. Geometry, idealizations, a few numbers, sometimes a figure that *is* the rest of the given. The law that turns those into an answer is almost never written in the stem. Intermediate variables are not introduced. Boundary conditions are left implicit in a sketch. The name of a standard result is frequently omitted even when the result is standard: the stem will describe a limiting case, a material class, a boundary condition, or an experimental signature without naming the theorem the choices are testing. The examinee supplies the identification.

Unused givens are a filter, not sloppiness. A mass that cancels; a kinematic datum when energy is asked at the point where one form vanishes; a relative speed when the asked quantity is a Lorentz invariant; an unused instrument knob. The student who starts writing a general formula using every symbol on the page has already missed the item. Kahn and Anderson’s advice to work symbolically — to name a given length as $R$ rather than to keep the number — is the solver’s version of the same design. Naming exposes unused data. A stem loaded with numbers that do not matter is a time tax on the student who uses them and a time gift to the student who does not. The omitted piece is frequently the point: if a mass appears and then cancels, the item is testing that cancellation, not the algebra of the orbit.

What is asked is rarely “solve the system.” It is which of these is true; which is not; which graph; which scaling; the value of a ratio; what happens immediately after a constraint is removed; what cannot be recovered. “Which of the following” is the grammar of selection among theories, not of calculation. On the later released forms it is the frame of about half the stems. Roman-numeral bundles appear sparingly and test the *boundary* of a principle — this law implies these two results and not a third — rather than pile three independent facts. “Most nearly” licenses order-of-magnitude physics and clean-number arithmetic. It is also a warning that a distractor will be the result of a missing factor, sitting close enough to tempt anyone who computed carefully with the wrong theory.

Stems are short on purpose. On the later released forms, once markup is stripped, median length sits under thirty words; older released papers run closer to forty or fifty. A long stem is almost always a shared setup amortized across two or three decisions, or an expression written out so that the actual work is trivial once parsed. Length is not difficulty. Parsing is. Do not add think-time by writing a longer stem. Shared-stem clusters are a pacing device: one drawing pays for two or three questions, and the later items change the verb, not the world. A set is a different time object than a standalone item. The reading tax is amortized only if the examinee does every item in the cluster.

## What the choices are for

Five options, always. They are not a menu of numbers jittered around a computed value. They are a diagnostic panel. Each wrong option is the output of a nearby wrong theory a hurried physicist would actually run; the right option is often the unique survivor of a filter the stem did not request.

Kahn and Anderson put this more sharply than anyone else writing in public: compared with AP Physics or the MCAT, “the testmakers throw in answer choices that are deliberately designed to mislead you.” They order those patterns by how much work they save, not by physics topic. That ordering is a claim about what an item is: the choices were built so that the cheapest legal filter is a solution path, not a luxury. A working physicist can often kill three options without finishing the arithmetic.

The failed nearby theories that recur are shadows of the one move the item wanted, not a taxonomy to copy. A scaling written linear where a square root belongs, or an intermediate offered as the final answer, is the kind of autopsy that belongs here. Numeric clusters are the outputs of those moves, spaced by factors of two, by decades, or by named wrong formulas ($\tfrac12 kT$, $kT$, $\tfrac32 kT$). Qualitative clusters are mutually exclusive accounts of the same situation, not vague synonyms. Verbal choices are the other physical story.

ETS builds the options so that “check a limit” is a legal path. Vanishing at an extreme, or a reversible process leaving the entropy of the universe unchanged rather than increased, can already kill choices the stem never asked anyone to filter.

A drill that fills distractors with nearby numbers is not speaking this dialect. Do not make the right answer the only dimensionally legal choice *and* the only limit-survivor *and* the only slogan match unless the other four are still specific wrong theories a rushed physicist would actually run.

Units-as-the-whole-item is a declining genre. Kahn dates pure dimensional-analysis items as common on older tests, fallen out of favor around 2000, at most a handful per test of the fully trivial mismatched-units kind. ETS still lists dimensional analysis under Laboratory Methods. Units-as-a-check remain first-class even when units-as-the-item recede. Do not write a paper of nothing but unit-veto items, and do not write a paper where units never kill a choice.

## The expensive look and the cheap door

The situation is allowed to look expensive. The intended path is cheap. This is not a personality of hard items. It is how almost every computational item is built.

A boundary-value costume invites an integral; the question that belongs on this exam asks for a quantity a substitution or a symmetry already *is*. A long formula is printed so that a limit recovers a named classical result in two substitutions. A constant is planted so that $\pi$ cancels. Pick numbers so the right identity lands on an integer or a small ratio; the numbers exist to snap, not to be realistic.

If the arithmetic is ugly, the wrong identity is in play. Conway’s production rule and the public consensus are the same sentence: numbers are chosen for easy arithmetic because no calculator is allowed. Physics Forums’ most repeated diagnostic is the inverse: going through a large amount of calculation was one sign that you had the problem wrong and should skip. Kahn treats algebra as a last resort: exhaust limiting cases and dimensional analysis first; if the algebra looks messy, skip it. Avoid kinematics when energy will do. Sanha Cheong’s current-format advice is the same: you cannot and should not do all the detailed calculations; dimensional analysis can almost always eliminate a few answers without any.

Figures do the same job as the numbers. A process loop looks like a thermodynamics problem and is an area. A landscape that looks like a boundary-value problem is a costume whose legal range of a coefficient already kills choices. A data plot asks for one physically meaningful feature — equal spacing, a power-law slope, an area that is a count — not a fit. Specify the figure first when the item is geometric or data-shaped, then write the shortest stem the figure does not already state.

This is the design move that textbook clones miss. A clone sets up the general problem the figure *could* support. The item supports one door: symmetry, conservation, a matching condition, an enclosed area, a dimensional veto, a cancellation, a vanishing. “Looks messy / solves clean” is how the item is built. Conway, who wrote items, lists conceptual solution and dimensional analysis as *intended* paths. A GRE item that has a five-minute solution and a thirty-second solution is designed so that the thirty-second path exists.

“Immediately after” is a clock inside the physics. The item is testing which variables are allowed to jump. That is the same cheap door: the expensive time-dependent problem is not being asked.

If an item takes four minutes of algebra, the examinee is off the designed path. The designed path is a single recognition, executed once. Then the item stops.

## One cognitive move, then stop

Multi-page derivations do not live here. An item that looks like rigid-body dynamics still collapses to $\tau=I\alpha$ with a standard $I$ and a moment arm from the figure. An item that looks like time-dependent perturbation theory is typically not present at all.

The move underneath the costume is one identification, executed once. Then the item stops. Factoid survey items are part of the voice, not a failure of it, and they sit at the same point value as a Lagrangian. When a calculation appears, it is one substitution into a named relation, or a two-line conservation. The numbers are round. The work is the identification.

There is a real disagreement about whether the exam is a calculation test or a trick-spotting test. One Physics Forums poster experienced “a lot of speedy calculations”; another said it was “spot the trick.” Conway says “short calculations, if any.” Brown says students fail the arithmetic without a calculator. All three describe the same form. Arithmetic is easy *after* the recognition step, and deadly if you skip it. Write items whose arithmetic is easy once the move is seen, and whose arithmetic is a trap if it is not.

## Costume is not content

A stem may dress itself in Pauli matrices, a Hamiltonian, a superconductor, an exotic two-body atom, a named resonance, monopoles in Maxwell’s equations, or a compact-object sentence. What is tested is the undergraduate slogan that makes the costume collapse.

An advanced condensed-matter costume collapses to a Maxwell boundary condition, with the microscopic mechanism sitting as a sophisticated neighbor. Quantum mechanics on this exam is structure, not technique. None of this is “solve the well.” All of it is “know what the well is for.”

ETS is not testing whether the examinee can derive the slogan. It is testing whether they possess it as a reflex and can refuse the sophisticated-looking wrong neighbor.

Conversely, items whose topics look elementary are not giveaways. They still require the right identity and the unused-given filter. An elementary statics costume whose unused geometric detail is the trap. Same design, cheaper costume. Elementary is not easy. Advanced is not deep.

Conway, from inside the committee: when he needed to consult a text, it was the freshman introductory textbook at least 80 percent of the time. “The single biggest mistake students make in studying for the GRE is to focus on too-advanced subjects.” This conflicts in emphasis, though not necessarily in arithmetic, with ETS’s own percentages: quantum plus atomic is nearly a quarter of the blueprint. Both can be true if “freshman” means the *style* — short, numerical, conceptual — and Griffiths-level set pieces are the upper-division content that still fits that style. Keep the tension as a writing rule. The costume may be upper-division. The demanded performance is not the interior of a graduate derivation. Do not test a graduate costume. Test the undergraduate move the costume reduces to.

What is not being tested, even when the topic looks like a qualifier: solving PDEs, contour integrals, perturbation series beyond first-order diagonal elements, experimental apparatus as *performance*. General relativity, quantum field theory, and most of many-body physics are out of scope. Special relativity is introductory. Kahn flags a Schwarzschild-radius mnemonic as a little too advanced; when such a sentence appears, it tests a freshman theorem wearing a compact-object name.

## The refusal

A distinctive cruelty of the form is that the sophisticated-looking setup is sometimes a demonstration that nothing happens, that something is independent of a parameter, or that a quantity cannot be recovered.

Independence is an answer. A parameter that never enters the dynamics cannot be recovered from the data. A uniform change that rescales two quantities together can leave a third unchanged. Symmetry can force a vector sum, a radiated power, or a force to vanish. On released forms these are often the hard tail. The trap is the examinee’s belief that a GRE item with a figure and three symbols must have a nonzero formula involving every symbol. The designed move is the refusal to compute.

The same spirit appears in inverted stems: which statement is NOT true; which CANNOT be calculated. One poison word is enough. The I/II/III bundles are a variant: the work is not computing, it is noticing the one false conjunct.

If zero, unchanged, independent, or cannot-be-determined never appears as a key in a generated set, the generator has not learned the refusal. If a stem looks expensive and the answer is a formula involving every symbol, ask whether symmetry already killed the sum.

## A figure is an argument, or it is not there

On the later released forms, roughly three items in ten carry a figure; on middle released forms, about a third. The figures are not illustrations of a stem that would stand alone. They are the data: circuit topology, process curves, spatial arrangements, a potential landscape, an apparatus plus its output plot.

They do three jobs, sometimes at once. The choices are pictures, so that recognizing a pattern is the whole problem: node versus antinode, high-pass versus low-pass, a field that vanishes in a region. Geometry that would take a paragraph is shown so the algebra is one line; the figure is a compression format, and reading it is the skill. Data to be read: a peak off a curve, then a named law; a log-log slope, then a power law.

What this exam does not do with figures: three-view engineering drawings, noisy experimental tables that must be fitted, or figures whose geometry has to be digitized. Angles that matter are labeled. Axes that matter are named. If a length is in the figure and not in the stem, it is the length you use. Unused knobs are bait. When there is no figure, the stem is short and the choices carry the content: five symbolic expressions, five qualitative claims, five graphs described in words.

A generator that writes a fully specified verbal stem and then “adds a figure” has the dependency backward. On this exam the figure is often what makes the item a GRE item rather than a paragraph of homework. If the figure is decoration, delete it or rebuild so the figure is the given.

Laboratory items are the same philosophy applied to instruments: lab as reading a representation, such as the shape of a distribution, not as operating a lab. Lab is a thin official slice, not an experiment.

## Time is a design parameter

One hundred seconds is not a consequence of the content. It is a design constraint that the content is built to satisfy. The 2023 cut conserved that number: 170 minutes for 100 items versus 120 minutes for 70 is 1.70 versus 1.71 minutes each. The format change does not buy think-time. It shortens the sitting. An item that cannot be entered through a named relation, a limit, a symmetry, or a units veto is off-style for this era, on both formats.

All questions are of equal value. ETS’s current advice: work rapidly without being careless; do not waste time pondering individual questions you find extremely difficult; consider a three-pass. Score is the number correct. “Nothing is subtracted from a score if you answer a question incorrectly. Therefore, to maximize your score it is better for you to guess at an answer than not to respond at all.” That last sentence is a real format change. Older paper forms used formula scoring, and much forum and departmental advice still assumes the penalty. The current practice book does not. Date-stamp guessing advice. For the sitting this document is written toward — October 2026 — always guess.

Forum disagreement about first-pass strategy is a disagreement about how to spend a shared budget, not about whether the budget is real. *Calculation time* is the enemy the item writer can choose to impose, and that the solver should refuse. Older “nail about 40” advice was a hundred-item-era score-calibration remark; under the current conversion, 40 correct out of 70 is roughly the 43rd percentile, not a high-score strategy. The principle survives. The raw-count target does not. ETS also tells examinees that no one is expected to be familiar with the content of every question.

Easy recognition items are scattered, including late; they are not concentrated in questions 1–10 as a confidence-builder. Difficulty is mixed through the booklet, not staged as easy-then-cliff. Shared stems and “most nearly” with widely spaced options are time gifts. Decoy quantities are a time tax. Vintage released forms were calibrated harder on average; middle forms sat near half. Harder, when the calibration tightens, means a narrower identification, not a longer calculation. The implied skill is not only physics. It is triage.

## The table of information is not the syllabus

ETS provides a table of constants, a few SI conversion factors, and a handful of moments of inertia. SI is used predominantly. Kahn’s judgment of the sheet is the right writing rule: it is “worse than useless: numerical values of constants you’ll never need, a couple random definitions, and three moments of inertia.” The intended knowledge is not “whatever is on the table.” Assume the examinee has the official table and still cannot survive without reflexes the table does not list: a small core of definitions, the ability to reconstruct standard results in a couple of lines, and a short list of scales that truly must be remembered — including scales that are *not* on the table. A result you can get to from more than one direction is a result you own.

There is a real tension in how coaches describe that knowledge. Kahn: derive the reconstructible layer from definitions; keep the must-remember list short. Brown and the CWRU flashcards: you do not have time to derive; students forget even basic formulas. Departmental pages split the same way: understand rather than memorize, then “know the content perfectly”; or, the goal is not to understand in the course sense but to do the problems fast. Conway required “a certain amount of memorization” of undergraduate equations and still insisted that many items are conceptual or dimensional. These are not the same philosophy. The items are written so that a derivable result *can* be reconstructed, and they are timed so that reconstruction often loses to recall. Do not write items whose solution is “look up the formula on the table and plug in.” Do not write items whose solution is a five-minute derivation of a standard result the examinee is expected to already possess as a reflex. The slogan is a reflex; the reconstruction is available as a backup; the clock prefers the reflex.

The item space is finite and repetitive: they can only ask so many questions about so many topics in so many different ways. Questions are recycled often, which is why so few exams have been released. Finite reuse is a production fact, not permission to clone. Write new situations that make the same *kind of demand*. Do not paraphrase an ETS stem closely enough that a person who has sat a released form would recognize it. Restage the move in a situation that is not the stock apparatus a textbook chapter uses for that slogan. If the only world you can stage is the canonical demo, you are cloning, not writing. Mathematical methods may appear as the language of a one-move item, not as a methods exam.

## What a GRE item refuses to be

It is not a qualifier problem. The demanded performance is never the interior of a graduate derivation. Conway granted the critic’s point that the test also measures work under pressure, memorization, and quick calculation, “good qualities for a physicist to have” but “by no means the only qualities required for a successful career.” He still argued that it “really does test knowledge about basic physics and the ability to analyze physical situations accurately.” Both halves are true of the instrument you are imitating. You are not writing a prelim.

It is not a long calculation. REA’s GRE Physics prep is the canonical negative example: too “heavy,” usually requiring a calculator, “completely unrepresentative.” Some buyers assumed that heavier problems would over-prepare. They over-prepare the wrong skill. “Harder” is not a synonym for “more GRE-like.”

It is not a textbook end-of-chapter workout with five numbers attached. Kahn himself is explicit that even *Conquering the Physics GRE* chapter-end problems are not GRE clones: they “don’t intend them to exactly replicate GRE questions in style and difficulty,” and they “may feature solutions with more steps than you would see on test day.” A drill-writing agent that imitates chapter problems will write homework with five choices. That is the failure mode this document exists to prevent.

It is not a complete undergraduate curriculum. ETS: no one is expected to know every question. A specialized item on this exam is still one slogan, one classification, or one one-line consequence, not a survey course in a stem. It is not partial-credit reasoning, calculator fluency, or lab technique as performance. The setting is almost always a standard undergraduate situation asked sideways, not a novel researchy vignette. It is not a sequence of equally hard problems or a confidence-building easy-then-cliff.

Antonio Levy’s species claim: “No matter how knowledgeable you are at physics, you have to learn to treat physics standardized tests as a whole different species of animal.” Conway and Kahn treat napkin methods as the *intended* solution. Physics Forums’ “spot the trick” phrasing treats them as a test-taking genre. Brown treats the same methods as lost undergraduate skills. Hold all three. The committee writes items that *can* be solved by principle, limit, and scale; the clock and the distractors *punish* the honest derivation; undergraduate instruction *no longer trains* the short path. That three-way tension is what a GRE Physics question is.

## The instrument is stable; the surface drifts

“GRE style” is time-dependent at the surface and stable underneath. Conway’s “short, to-the-point” describes the form the committee was writing in the late 2000s, which is closer to 2008 / 2017 / 2024 than to 1986. Kahn: only the 2001, 2008, and 2017 released exams are representative of the *current content* of the test; the 1980s–1990s forms are useful muscle but not a style match. Brown: older tests have much longer problems, so do them without worrying about time. Other coaches: the 1990s tests are harder; if short on time, prioritize recent. These claims are compatible if “style match” and “skill building” are distinguished. They conflict if a drill agent treats a 1986 paper as a model of 2026 stem craft.

What is stable: five choices; qualitative decisions mixed with short formulas; figures as arguments; unused givens as filters; autopsy distractors; the one-move aesthetic; null and unchanged and cannot as first-class keys; engineered numbers; advanced vocabulary as costume; about a hundred seconds; the same coarse topic weights. Recurring demands reappear because the undergraduate slogans are few — not because those situations are a catalog you should clone.

What drifts is surface and calibration. Vintage papers are more talkative: more EXCEPT, more Roman-numeral, slightly longer stems, a harder average calibration, slightly more graduate costume with the same elementary move. Middle forms shift mildly toward classification stems. Later papers run shorter stems and use “which of the following” more densely. Pure units-as-item recedes. The 70-item sitting is a trim of the 100-item sitting, not a new species. Write for the current clock and grammar: short stem, five autopsies, one move, about a hundred seconds, no calculator, no guessing penalty. Let vintage and middle teach the philosophy. Do not let them set the stem length or the EXCEPT density of a 2026 drill.

## How to know you have written one

Before emitting an item, audit it. These are tests of form, not templates. If you find yourself asking whether this is “an Atwood-machine item,” you have left the philosophy and entered a catalog.

Would a homework grader accept a page of algebra as the intended solution? If yes, the item is not done. The intended path must be a named identity, a limit, a symmetry, a conservation selector, a units veto, a pattern in a figure, or a one-line substitution after an identification. If you cannot state the one cognitive move in a sentence, you have written a workout.

Does the stem use every given? If yes, you are missing the unused-given filter — unless the item is a pure recognition with almost no givens, which is legal and should appear sometimes. Is there a theatrical setup with a cheap door: a cancellation, a vanishing, an independence, a matching condition, an enclosed area, a dimensional veto? If the setup could support a general problem and you have asked the general problem, you have written the clone. Hide the instruction. Name the world, not the theorem.

Do the four wrong choices name failed theories, or are they a spread around a number? If a competent hurried physicist would not actually produce a given distractor, replace it. Would this item be decidable, or at least reducible to two choices, by a limiting-case check, a dimensional veto, or a symmetry argument the stem did not explicitly request? If the only path is grinding the interior, you have missed the designed second route. If *every* item in a set dies on units alone, you are writing the declining pure-units genre, not a paper.

If you have dressed the stem in graduate vocabulary, does the demanded performance collapse to an undergraduate slogan? If the examinee must actually run the advanced machinery, you have written a qualifier problem. Could the right answer be zero, unchanged, independent of a parameter, or cannot-be-determined? If your keys never say so, you have not learned the refusal.

Is the figure an argument — topology, shape-as-answer, data from which one feature is extracted — or decoration of a stem that would stand alone? If decoration, delete it or rebuild so the figure is the given. Can a prepared examinee who sees the move finish in about a hundred seconds without a calculator, with arithmetic that snaps or vanishes? If not, the numbers are wrong or the move is not one move. Do not add think-time by lengthening the stem.

Would this item be at home as an unmarked textbook exercise, an REA calculator problem, a qualifier derivation, or a chapter-end workout with five choices glued on? Then it is not a GRE item. Attaching five numbers to a derivation does not convert it. “Harder” does not convert it. More advanced vocabulary does not convert it.

A long run of items in one subject is a quiz, not a form. Respect the committee’s content mix at the level of a sitting, not at the level of every short drill. In any sitting-length set, include some items that never want a number, some pure recognition, and at least one refusal key. Mix difficulty. Do not stage easy-then-cliff. Do not assume formula scoring.

If the draft fails several of these tests, it is a textbook problem. Rewrite from the move. Name the principle. Stage just enough world that the principle applies. Withhold the procedure. Leave at least one given that a grinding solver will try to use. Plant four autopsies of real wrong moves. Make the cheap door visible to someone who knows the slogan and invisible to someone who starts deriving. Let the right answer sometimes be that nothing happens. Then stop.
