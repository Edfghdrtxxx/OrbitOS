# Gerunds

## Context

Forked mid-parent [[2026-08-15-1050 TOEFL complete-word forms]] Turn 12. Learner asked for gerund rules after overgeneralizing “gerund is not valid behind an adjective” on *prevent accidental plagiarism*. Vault already has [[Gerund]] (noun *-ing*; vs participle; vs infinitive; after prepositions). Parent **#3** (*remarkably* / *innovation*) is parked. Diagnose the live collision before teaching rules.

---

### Turn 1 — 2026-08-15 (learner)

> Teach me gerund rules as well because I sometimes still feel confused about them.

### Tutor

We split this out. *-ing* is a spelling, not a part of speech. The same letters can be a noun (*Swimming is tiring*), a verb (*I am swimming*), or a describer (*a swimming child*). Your last rule treated every *-ing* as one object that cannot follow an adjective. That is why the rule broke.

I will not dump a rule list until I know which collision is live.

Which fog is the one that actually bites you?

1. When *-ing* is a name vs still a doing-word (the *accidental killing* / *accidentally killing* split)
2. Gerund vs *to do* (*enjoy swimming* / *want to swim*)
3. Something else — name it

---

### Turn 2 — 2026-08-15 (learner)

> All, my mind was tangled.

### Tutor

Then we sequence. Not all at once. Collision 1 first — it is the floor the others stand on. Gerund vs *to do* comes after you can see when *-ing* is a name.

Same spelling. Three jobs. Ask which job *this* sentence hired.

- **Name** (this is the gerund): sits where a noun sits. *Swimming is hard.* *I enjoy swimming.*
- **Doing-word** (verb): *I am swimming.* *He was accidentally killing time.*
- **Describer**: *a swimming child.* Parked until 1 is solid.

The tell for name vs doing-word is the neighbor:

- adjective + *-ing* → the *-ing* is being treated as a **name** (*the accidental killing*)
- adverb + *-ing* → the *-ing* is still **doing** (*accidentally killing*)

Two sentences. In which one is *killing* a name, and what neighbor told you?

1. *The accidental killing shocked the court.*
2. *He was accidentally killing time.*

---

### Turn 3 — 2026-08-15 (learner)

> 1. Is a name. It plays the role of the subject.
> 2. Is still doing.

### Tutor

Yes. Both.

You used the stronger tell: **what slot it sits in.** Subject = name. The neighbors agree — *accidental* (adjective) in 1, *accidentally* (adverb) in 2 — but the slot is enough by itself.

A name has more than one chair. Subject is one. Object is the other.

*I enjoy swimming.*

Is *swimming* a name or a doing-word — and which chair is it in?

---

### Turn 4 — 2026-08-15 (learner)

> doing-word. I enjoy the process of doing such things.

### Tutor

That is the usual trap, and it is about meaning, not the chair.

Yes: *swimming* names an activity. Gerunds exist for that — a **name of a doing**. The doing-feel does not make it the verb of the sentence.

*Enjoy* is already the verb. It needs a thing after it:

- *I enjoy music.*
- *I enjoy this.*
- *I enjoy swimming.*

Same chair. *Music* is not a doing-word. *Swimming* sits where *music* sits. That chair is **object** = name.

Doing-word looks like *I am swimming* — *am* + *-ing*, subject doing it now.

Can you swap *swimming* for *music* in *I enjoy swimming* and keep the sentence shape? If yes, which chair is that?

---

### Turn 5 — 2026-08-15 (learner)

> Yes, I can swap and keep the sentence shape. A name.

### Tutor

Yes. Object chair. *Enjoy* is the verb; *swimming* is the thing enjoyed — a name of a doing.

Third name chair, then collision 1 is complete. A preposition also needs a noun after it, so it takes a gerund, not a bare verb.

- *She is good at math.*
- *She is good at swimming.*

Same chair after *at*.

*She is interested in ___ Japanese.*

What goes in — *learn*, *learning*, or *is learning* — and which chair is that?

---

### Turn 6 — 2026-08-15 (learner)

> learning. Name + object chair

### Tutor

Yes. *Learning*. Name. The object is of *in*, not of *interested* — same family as the *enjoy* chair, just after a preposition.

Collision 1 is complete. Three name chairs: subject, object of a verb, object of a preposition. After a preposition English wants that name (*in learning*), not *in learn* and not *in is learning*.

One leftover job from the opening map, then we touch gerund vs *to do*.

*The swimming child waved.*

Is *swimming* a name, a doing-word, or a describer? Try the swap: can *music* sit there?

---

### Turn 7 — 2026-08-15 (learner)

> A describer. And music can't sit there.

### Tutor

Yes. *Swimming* describes *child*. Swap dies, so it is not in a name chair. (It is also not *is swimming* — no *be*. Describer only.)

Three *-ing* jobs you can now tell apart: name / doing-word / describer.

Collision 2: English has **two** name-forms of an action. The chair test will not pick which one.

- *-ing* name: *enjoy swimming*
- *to* + verb: *want to swim*

The verb on the left hires one of them. That is a fact about the verb, not about the activity.

Fill both:

1. *I enjoy ___ (swim).*
2. *I want ___ (swim).*

---

### Turn 8 — 2026-08-15 (learner)

> I enjoy swimming
> I want to swim

### Tutor

Yes. *Enjoy* hires the *-ing* name. *Want* hires *to*.

Some left-hand verbs hire **both**, and the meaning splits. That is the trap, not the *enjoy* / *want* pair.

*Stop* is one of them:

- *I stopped smoking.*
- *I stopped to smoke.*

In plain words: what changed between those two? Do not just rename the forms.

---

### Turn 9 — 2026-08-15 (learner)

> The meanings of the two sentences are totally different. The former represents no smoking anymore. The latter represents the start of smoking.

### Tutor

First sentence: yes. *Stopped smoking* = quit that activity. The *-ing* name is the thing you ended.

Second sentence is close and then slips. *Stopped to smoke* is not “smoking begins now” as a new habit. *To* here means **in order to**. You stopped *something else* so that you could smoke.

The sentence hides the something else. A person walking home *stopped to smoke*: they stopped walking, purpose = smoke.

What did the person stop in sentence 2 — smoking, or a different action?

---

### Turn 10 — 2026-08-15 (learner)

> stopped a different action

### Tutor

Yes. *Stop + -ing* = end that activity. *Stop + to* = pause something else **in order to** do this.

Same split, new verb. *Remember* also hires both:

- *I remembered locking the door.*
- *I remembered to lock the door.*

What changed? In which one is the locking already in the past when the remembering happens?

---

### Turn 11 — 2026-08-15 (learner)

> The time that remembering action happened changed. The first one.

### Tutor

First one: yes. *Remembered locking* = a memory of an event that was already done.

The other half slips. Both sentences use *remembered* — the remembering is in the past in both. What moves is the **locking**, not the remembering.

- *-ing*: locking first, then you recall the picture
- *to*: you recall the duty, then you lock

In sentence 2, which comes first — the remembering, or the locking?

---

### Turn 12 — 2026-08-15 (learner)

> I understand now. The remembering comes first, then the locking.

### Tutor

Yes. *Remember + -ing* = a picture of something already done. *Remember + to* = a duty that still needs doing.

That is enough gerund for this fork. You can now tell:

- three *-ing* jobs (name / doing-word / describer)
- three name chairs (subject, verb-object, preposition-object)
- the left-hand verb hires *-ing* or *to*
- two meaning splits: *stop*, *remember*

A short hire-set to keep — not a list to memorize tonight:

- *-ing* only: *enjoy, avoid, mind, finish, suggest, consider*
- *to* only: *want, decide, hope, plan, refuse, expect*
- both, meaning splits: *stop, remember, forget, try*
