### Tutor

Here is the breakdown of your 5 items, isolating the exact conversational and grammatical mechanics behind each one:

---

### 1. Pragmatic Register & Conversational Role
- **Your deduction:** *"taking reservations implies a waiter-customer interaction, not between friends."*
- **Verdict:** **100% correct.** 
- **Rule:** In English conversational tests, watch the **speaker role / speech act**. Service providers (restaurants, hotels, airlines) *"take reservations"*, while customers *"make a reservation"* or ask *"Do you take reservations?"*. Friends discussing plans cannot "take reservations" from each other.

---

### 2. Alternative Questions: "Would you rather A or B?"
- **Your question:** *"Do we never use 'I would' to answer 'Would you rather A or B?'"*
- **Rule:** **Yes, you can never answer a bare "I would."**
- **Why:** 
  - *"Would you rather A or B?"* is an **alternative choice question** (an "Or-question"), not a Yes/No question.
  - A bare *"I would"* is an elliptical response that only works when there is a single predicate to refer back to (*"Would you ever study abroad?" $\to$ "I would."* = *"I would study abroad."*).
  - For an $A \text{ or } B$ choice, *"I would"* leaves the sentence incomplete: *I would which one?*
  - **Valid responses:**
    - Stating preference: *"I'd rather take the bus."* / *"I prefer the train."*
    - Indifference: *"Either is fine with me."* / *"I don't mind."* / *"Neither, actually."*

---

### 3. The "Ghost Sentence" (Mental Completion) Trap
- **Your thought:** *Chose D ("Did you want to go for a swim?") by mentally prepending "Yes."*
- **The Trap:** Never rescue an option by inventing a missing lead-in word (*"Yes," "No," "Because"*). 
- **Rule:** The option must function as a standalone, direct, or pragmatically cooperative turn. An isolated question like *"Did you want to go for a swim?"* only works if the original prompt explicitly set up swimming or an invitation (e.g., *"I'm packing my towel and heading out now"*).

---

### 4. Presupposition Denial vs. Irrelevant Subject Shift (Question 11)
- **Prompt:** *"Do you know where Tom parked his car?"*
- **Your choice:** *"I don't have a car."* (Assuming: *"Because I don't have a car, I don't know."*)
- **Why "I don't have a car" is wrong (Subject Shift):** The speaker asked about **Tom's** car and Tom's location. Whether *you* own a car has no logical connection to whether you saw or know where Tom parked.
- **Why "He usually takes the bus" is correct (Presupposition Denial):**
  - The question presupposes that **Tom drove and parked a car today**.
  - In conversational pragmatics, an indirect answer often **defeats the false premise**: *"He usually takes the bus"* $\implies$ *"Tom probably didn't drive today, so there is no parked car to look for."*

---

### 5. Specific Event vs. Recurring Habit / Schedule (Question 1)
- **Prompt:** *"Are you going to the lecture tomorrow?"*
- **Why Option B ("Yes, two times a week") is wrong (Frequency Trap):**
  - *"Tomorrow"* refers to a **single, specific point in time / event**.
  - *"Two times a week"* expresses **frequency / recurring habit** (answering *"How often do you go?"* or *"How often is the class held?"*). You cannot use a recurring routine to answer whether you are attending a specific event tomorrow.
- **Why Option A ("I have a project I need to finish") is correct:**
  - In campus English, declining an invitation or stating non-attendance is standardly expressed via an **indirect conflict / excuse**: *"I have a project I need to finish"* $\implies$ *"No, I can't attend because I'm busy finishing a project."*
  - *(Note on third-party erratum: The practice material's key marked (C) "Sure, I took lots of notes", which contains an undeniable tense clash (`tomorrow` vs. `took`). In genuine ETS design, (A) is the grammatically and pragmatically valid response).*

- **Prompt:** `Isn't the pool open today?`
- **Options:**

  - (B) *We have a replacement.*
  - (C) *I don't think they have it yet.*
  - (D) *Did you want to go for a swim?*
- **The Counter-Question Pattern:**
  - You picked **D**, which is **the correct answer**!
  - Your confusion was only about *why* it worked without adding a phantom *"Yes."*
  - In English conversation, responding to a question with a **counter-question about the speaker's motive / purpose** is a standard standalone speech act:
    - Speaker A: *"Isn't the pool open today?"*
    - Speaker B: *"Did you want to go for a swim?"* (Meaning: *"Why do you ask? Were you planning to swim?"*)
  - No preface (*"Yes"* / *"No"*) is needed. The question itself serves as the entire cooperative response.

---

### Summary of TOEFL Best Response Rules
1. **Speaker Role / Register:** Institutional service acts (*take reservations*) cannot be spoken between friends/colleagues.
2. **Alternative ($A \text{ or } B$) Choice:** Never takes a bare *"Yes"* or *"I would"*; requires choice or indifference (*Whatever's more convenient*).
3. **Counter-Question (Motive):** Checking status/schedule often elicits an inquiry into *why* you are asking (*Did you want to go for a swim?*).
4. **Presupposition Defeat:** Legitimate indirect answers challenge the hidden premise (*He usually takes the bus*), unlike subject-shifting distractors (*I don't have a car*).
5. **Time Horizon Match:** Specific future events (*tomorrow / tonight*) reject frequency/habit answers (*two times a week / every evening*), taking conflict excuses instead (*I have a project I need to finish*).

### Error-Prone Patterns (Mastery Summary)

1. **The Keyword Category Trap (Affirmative Head + Category Shift):**
   - **How it traps:** Distractors open with an agreeable *"Yes,"* and borrow a word from the same thematic universe (calculus textbook $\to$ notebooks; biology lab $\to$ microscope; pool $\to$ swim), tempting you to treat it as an answer.
   - **The Rule:** An answer must resolve the specific predicate in question. Shared campus vocabulary does not make a sentence relevant.

2. **Counter-Questions on Motive (Standalone Speech Acts):**
   - **How it traps:** You feel an urge to mentally prepend *"Yes."* to justify a question as a reply.
   - **The Rule:** In spoken English, asking *why* someone is inquiring about an object, status, or schedule (*"Are you taking his course?" / "Did you want to go for a swim?" / "Are you trying to run that PCR experiment?"*) is a completely natural, standalone cooperative response.

3. **Presupposition Defeat vs. Irrelevant Subject Shift:**
   - **How it traps:** When asked about a third party's action (*Where did Tom park? / What time does David's flight land?*), distractor options shift the subject to *your* personal circumstances (*I don't have a car / I took a flight*).
   - **The Rule:** Valid indirect responses defeat the underlying assumption about the target subject (*He takes the bus / He drove*). Reject any shift to the listener's unrelated life.
