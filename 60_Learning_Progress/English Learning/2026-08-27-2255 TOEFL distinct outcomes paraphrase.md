# TOEFL: distinct outcomes paraphrase

## Context

Listening/reading review screenshot (complete item text):

- **QUESTION:** What problem does Professor Park identify in Emma's original topic?
- **YOUR ANSWER** (incorrect, red): Her proposed survey would take too long to complete
- **CORRECT ANSWER** (green): Her definition of mental health includes distinct outcomes
- **WHY:** Professor Park points out that anxiety, depression, loneliness, and self-esteem are separate research areas. Treating all of them as mental health makes Emma's original question too broad.

Named gap: WHY vs CORRECT. Skip context reconstruction (English/TOEFL). Direct mapping of the paraphrase, then one lock question.

---

### Turn 1 — 2026-08-27 (learner)

> I don't understand the relationship between the clue and the answer. Help me bridge the logical gap.

### Tutor

The WHY and the green answer are the same complaint in two dialects. Park is not talking about survey length. He is talking about **one label covering four different measured things**.

```
Emma's box, labeled "mental health"
┌──────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ anxiety · depression · loneliness · self-esteem │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

Park: those four are **separate research areas**. Each is its own thing a study would measure.

Test English then does a word-for-word swap:

- separate → **distinct**
- thing a study measures → **outcome**
- stuffing them all into the word *mental health* → **her definition includes** them

So "too broad" here means the **category is mixed**, not "the questionnaire would take forever." That time reading is why the red choice is tempting.

If Emma kept a long survey but measured **only depression**, would Park still make this same objection?

### Turn 2 — 2026-08-27 (learner)

> Is the word-for-word swap a safe stragety in taking TOEFL tests?
> If Emma kept a long survey bu measured only depression, Park would not still make this same objection
> /evolve-skills If there are images, record the complete corresponding content in the context section.

### Tutor

Yes. That objection is about a mixed category, not about how long the survey is. One outcome (depression) → the definition is no longer too broad → Park drops this complaint.

Word-for-word swap is **not** a safe test strategy. I used it here as a *decoder* for this one pair, not as a method you should run on every item.

On TOEFL, the dangerous pattern is the opposite of what it feels like:

- **Same words, wrong relationship** → often a trap. Your red choice took "too broad" and turned it into "takes too long."
- **Different words, same judgment** → often the key. "Separate research areas" became "distinct outcomes."

The safe move: lock Park's *judgment* (what's wrong and why), then pick the option that restates that judgment. Matching nouns is optional and often misleading.

Try this: an option that lists anxiety, depression, loneliness, and self-esteem and says Emma's topic is well focused — do you take it because the nouns match, or reject it because the judgment is wrong?

The reject is right. The AND gate is not what I was suggesting — and it would have thrown out the green answer on this item.

Judgment is the only required gate. Word overlap is neither required nor sufficient:

```
judgment match?   word overlap?   take it?
     yes               no          YES   ← this item's green ("distinct outcomes")
     yes               yes         YES   ← lucky overlap, still fine
     no                yes         NO    ← the trap you just rejected
     no                no          NO
```

The swap list (separate → distinct, measured thing → outcome) was only a decoder for *why* the green restates Park. On the test you do not need those words to appear, and you do not need a second gate.

Apply that table to the actual key: "Her definition of mental health includes distinct outcomes." It matches Park's judgment but does **not** reuse anxiety / depression / loneliness / self-esteem. Would your AND gate keep this option or throw it out?

### Tutor

Keep it now — that's the table locked. The first miss was a different failure: you had no meaning for the green sentence, so judgment-match had nothing to match against. The time trap was the only option that still made sense.

On a research item, **outcome** ≈ a thing the study measures. **Distinct** ≈ separate, not one mixed pile. So "her definition includes distinct outcomes" ≈ she stuffed several different measured things into one label. That *is* Park's "too broad."

### Turn 5 — 2026-08-27 (learner)

> The thing the study measures or the critical thing related to objective.

### Tutor

Keep the first clause. Drop the *or*.

**Outcome** = the thing you record: a score, a state, a count. Anxiety, depression, loneliness, self-esteem are four outcomes.

**Objective** = the aim of the study: what you are trying to find out. It is not itself a measured thing.

They sit next to each other — you measure outcomes *in order to* hit an objective — but TOEFL will punish treating them as the same word. "Related to the objective" is true in a loose sense and too wide to use as a translation.

A paper says: *The study's objective was to test whether sleep affects depression.* Which word is *depression* here — objective or outcome?

This thread is done. What you can now do:

- **Too broad** on a topic item = mixed category, not a long survey.
- **Keep the option** if it restates the professor's judgment, even when the nouns differ. Noun overlap with the wrong judgment is a trap.
- **Outcome** = the thing the study measures. **Objective** = the aim. Do not use "related to the objective" as a translation of *outcome*.
- **Distinct outcomes** in Emma's item = several different measured things stuffed into one label. That *is* Park.
