# since / as / because

## Context

New thread. Learner asked how to use *since*, *as*, and *because*. No prior session on this trio. *because* already appears correctly in TOEFL AD models; *since* as a time marker is in [[Present Perfect]] (*has lived … since 2020*). Diagnosis turn: locate whether the collision is “they all mean why” or the second life of *since* / *as* (time).

---

### Turn 1 — 2026-08-14 (learner)

> /learn How to use "since", "as", "becasue".

### Tutor

These three overlap as reason-words, but they are not copies of each other.

**Because** is the dedicated “why” word. If you only need a reason and you want zero ambiguity, this is the default.

The trap is *since* and *as*: each has another life.

- *I have lived here **since** 2020.* → starting point in time, not a reason
- *I stayed inside **as** it started to rain.* → this can be time (“while”) or reason (“because”)

When you write, do these three feel interchangeable as “why” words, or is the real collision *since* / *as* also meaning time?

### Tutor

Yes — that swap is dead. Nesting is gone.

*Since* has private territory *as* cannot enter: **from a past point until now**. That is why *since 2020* works and *as 2020* does not.

The picture is overlap, not containment: they share the why-core; each keeps leftover space.

### Turn 4 — 2026-08-14 (learner)

> As a student, it is indispensable for us to catch up with frontier AI tools.

### Tutor

That’s a real leftover. *As a student* = **role** (in that capacity). *Since a student* means nothing. So *as* has private space.

Notice the shape: *as* + a noun. That is not the same machine as *as* + a full clause (*as I am a student* — that one can be why).

*Because* cannot take that noun slot either: *because a student* is dead. *Because* lives in the why-core and wants a clause.

Side note, not this lesson: *As a student* wants *I*, not *us* — the role and the person have to match.

One apply — leftover vs why-core:

1. ___ a physicist, she spends most of her time on detectors.
2. She stayed late ___ the detector still had a leak.

Which word for (1), which for (2)?

### Turn 5 — 2026-08-14 (learner)

> As a physicsit, she spends most of her time on detectors
> She stayed late since the detector still had a leak.

### Tutor

**(1) is locked.** *As a physicist* — role, leftover. That swap-test is done.

**(2) is not dead.** The why-core admits *because*, *since*, and *as*. You picked a legal word.

It is the marked pick, though. *Because* is the dedicated why-word: it puts the reason in the spotlight (the leak is the news). Causal *since* usually treats the reason as already known, and it is happier at the front: *Since the detector still had a leak, she stayed late.*

Try the Why-test:

> Why did she stay late?

Answer with *Because…* and then with *Since…*. Which one sounds like an answer to the question?

### Tutor

Yes. *Because* answers Why?; *Since…, she stayed late* is the given-reason shape.

Map you now have:

- **because** — dedicated why-word. Reason is the news. Answers Why?
- **since** — leftover: from a past point until now. In the why-core: reason already known; usually fronted.
- **as** — leftover you named: role (*as a physicist*). Also sits in the why-core like *since*, but we have not trained that yet.

One apply, your own work, two sentences:

1. Reason is the news → *because*
2. Reason is already known; you want the consequence → fronted *since*

Keep them short.

### Tutor

Same leftover-vs-core idea, now inside *as* + a clause.

*As* + clause has two jobs:

- why-core: given reason, like fronted *since*
- leftover: *while* (two things at the same time)

The swap test is the same tool you already used.

1. *As I walked into the lab, the detector started beeping.*
2. *As the beam was already off, we shut down the run.*

### Turn 10 — 2026-08-14 (learner)

> The first one still works if I swap as -> while, because while places emphasis on the persistence. THe second one still works if I swap as -> since, because there is causation.

### Tutor

The sort is right. (1) *while*, (2) *since*.

One fix on the reason for (1): it is not persistence. *While* here means **same time** — the walking and the beeping overlap. The walk can be short. *As I walked in* is a moment, not a long-lasting state.

(2) is the why-core sibling of *since*: the beam being off is already on the table; shutting down is the point.

The trap is a sentence where both doors open:

Can you get both a *while* reading and a *since* reading? If you wanted only one of those meanings, which word would you write instead of *as*?

### Turn 11 — 2026-08-14 (learner)

> Yes, I can obtain both a while reading and a since reading. Instead of as, I would rather choose while. Because the reason could not already be on the table.

### Tutor

Both doors open: that’s the trap. The next move is not to default to *while*.

The causal door is still open. In a lab sentence, *the temperature dropped* is a very natural reason for *the reaction slowed*. It can already be on the table (you’re looking at the plot) or it can be news. *As* doesn’t tell the reader which job you meant.

You replace *as* with the word for the door **you** want:

- same time → *while*
- cause, and the drop is news → *because*
- cause, and the drop is already on the table → *since*

Two fills, same sentence:

1. You mean the drop **caused** the slowdown, and the drop is news.  
2. You mean **during** the drop.
