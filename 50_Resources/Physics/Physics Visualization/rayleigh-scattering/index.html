Rayleigh scattering: why the daytime sky is blue and the sunset is red

A spectral simulation of sunlight crossing Earth's atmosphere. Air molecules scatter light with a cross section that grows as λ⁻⁴, so blue light is scattered about 4.5 times more than red. In daytime you look at that scattered light, which is blue. At sunset you look at the light that is left after a path through the air that is tens of times longer, which is red.

Each pixel integrates the light scattered along its line of sight at 16 wavelengths (380–740 nm), then converts the spectrum to colour with the CIE 1931 observer. The mountains are 5, 19 and 58 km away, so their haze shows the same scattering (aerial perspective).

The path of sunlight to you · Monte Carlo photons, cross-section along the Sun's direction

Each photon follows the line from the top of the atmosphere to you and, at every 2 km step, is scattered with probability 1 − exp(−β·ρ(h)·Δs) using the same coefficients as the sky render. Scattered photons continue in a new direction drawn from the Rayleigh phase function; those are the photons that make the sky glow.

1Small molecules prefer short waves

An N₂ or O₂ molecule is about 1000 times smaller than a wavelength of light. The light's electric field drives its electrons as a tiny oscillating dipole, and a dipole radiates power ∝ ω⁴ ∝ λ⁻⁴.

Here the exact formula gives β(450 nm)/β(650 nm) = –; a pure λ⁻⁴ law gives (650/450)⁴ = 4.35. The small excess comes from the dispersion of air's refractive index and from the King factor.

2Daytime: you see the scattered light

Looking away from the Sun, every photon you receive was scattered toward you. Blue is scattered most, so the sky is blue. The sky is not violet because sunlight contains less violet, the eye is less sensitive there, and the violet is partly scattered away before it reaches the lower air.

Zenith optical depth at 550 nm is only –, so most sunlight still reaches the ground and the Sun looks white.

3Sunset: you see what is left

Near the horizon the beam crosses about – times as much air as at the zenith. Transmittance is exp(−τ·m), so blue, with a larger τ, is removed first. The Sun and the clouds or mountains it lights turn orange and red.

Switch to Away from Sun at sunset or in twilight to see Earth's shadow rising in the east, with the pink band above it lit by reddened sunlight.

What the simulation computes

Cross section per molecule (Bodhaine et al. 1999, eq. 22), with the refractive index of standard air $n_s$ from Peck & Reeder (1972) and the King depolarization factor $F_K$ from Bates (1984):

$$\sigma(\lambda)=\frac{24\pi^3}{\lambda^4 N_s^2}\left(\frac{n_s^2-1}{n_s^2+2}\right)^2F_K(\lambda),\qquad N_s=\frac{p_0}{k_BT_0}$$

Scattering coefficient with an exponential atmosphere. The scale height $H_R=k_BT_0/(m g)=8.43$ km makes the column $N_sH_R$ equal to the hydrostatic column $p_0/(mg)$:

$$\beta_R(\lambda,h)=N_s\,\sigma(\lambda)\,e^{-h/H_R},\qquad P_R(\theta)=\frac{3}{16\pi}\left(1+\cos^2\theta\right)$$

Single scattering along each view ray (Nishita et al. 1993), with aerosols (Cornette–Shanks phase, $g=0.8$) and ozone absorption included in the extinction:

$$L(\lambda)=E_\odot(\lambda)\int_0^{t_\text{max}}\!\Big[\beta_R P_R(\theta)+\beta_M P_M(\theta)\Big]\, T_{\text{eye}\to x}(\lambda)\,T_{x\to\odot}(\lambda)\,dt$$ $$T(\lambda)=\exp\!\Big(-\!\int\big[\beta_R+\beta_{M,\text{ext}}+\beta_{O_3}\big]\,ds\Big)$$

Multiple scattering (Hillaire 2020): light scattered two or more times is treated as isotropic. At each altitude and Sun angle, a table stores the second-order radiance $L_2$ and the fraction $f_{ms}$ of light that scatters again; the sum of all orders is a geometric series, and $\sigma_s\Psi_{ms}$ is added at every step:

$$\Psi_{ms}=L_2\left(1+f_{ms}+f_{ms}^2+\dots\right)=\frac{L_2}{1-f_{ms}}$$

Colour: $X,Y,Z=\sum_i L(\lambda_i)\,\{\bar x,\bar y,\bar z\}_i\,\Delta\lambda$ with the CIE 1931 functions (analytic fit of Wyman et al. 2013), then linear sRGB, white-balanced so that the Sun above the atmosphere is neutral, then exposure and a hue-preserving tone curve. Luminance is $683\,Y$ in cd/m².

Limits of the model

  • Multiple scattering is approximate: isotropic, a function of altitude and Sun angle only, with a 10% grey ground. Untick it to see single scattering alone: Earth's shadow in twilight then turns black.
  • Spherical Earth, exponential air and aerosol profiles, a 10–40 km triangular ozone layer of 300 Dobson units, no clouds, no refraction (the real Sun stays visible about 0.5° below the geometric horizon).
  • The Monte Carlo panel scatters photons within the drawing plane; the sky render uses the full 3-D phase function.
