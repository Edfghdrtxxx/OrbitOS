Slide 2 · the framework

All the physics hides in one number: δₗ(E)

Asymptotic radial wave function:  uℓ(r) → sin(kr − ℓπ/2 + δℓ(E))  ·  cross-section σℓ(E) = 4π(2ℓ+1)/k² · sin²δℓ(E)

Phase shift δ
What this shows
A free wave (green, dashed) and a wave that has passed through a scatterer (blue). After the interaction, the two differ only by a constant phase δ — that's the full story of scattering, packaged in one number per partial wave.
§ 1 · partial-wave expansion
Everything we compute for the next four slides is δ(E) or something derived from it. The sign of δ tells you attraction vs repulsion; jumps of π tell you resonances; the low-E slope tells you the scattering length. One number, many faces.

§ 2 · Breit-Wigner form
The left panel is δ(E); the right is sin²(δ−δ_bg), i.e. the shape of the partial-wave cross-section. Shrink Γ to sharpen the resonance. Move δ_bg up or down to show how a non-resonant background hides the peak — crucially, the π jump in δ is still there even when σ looks featureless. § 3 · spherical square well
Left: the blue wave is the actual u(r); the green dashed wave is the V=0 reference. Inside the well the wavelength shortens (higher kinetic energy) and outside it shifts by δ. Right: δ₀(E) scanned for the current (D, W, ℓ). Crank D up to see resonance-like steps where a new partial-wave bound state is about to appear. The extraction is a discrete zero-crossing comparison — simple, and matches the sheet to ~1%.

§ 4 · scattering length
This is the signature of low-energy scattering: the whole behaviour collapses into a single length a. The slope/intercept construction makes it visually obvious. Slide D up — as the first bound state is about to appear (D ≈ π²/4 for unit W), a shoots to +∞, flips through −∞, and the next branch starts. That's the Efimov/Feshbach/halo-nucleus physics in one animation.

§ 5 · shell-barrier resonance
Exact geometry from the source spreadsheet: a particle lives in the inner cavity 0 < r < W₁, can only escape by tunnelling through a finite-width shell barrier W₁ ≤ r ≤ W₁+B. At the resonance energy E ≈ 1.617 (for the default W₁=2, B=1, H=5), a half-wavelength just fits the cavity and the inside amplitude surges. Raise H or B and the resonance sharpens — longer lifetime, narrower Γ. This is the radial analogue of α-decay: quasi-bound state, escape rate set by the tunnelling probability through the shell.

Slide 7 · putting it together

One formalism, four faces of δ

Every demo on the previous four slides is one slice of the S-matrix S(E) = e2iδ(E). Its poles classify everything we saw.

Bound state

Pole of S on the positive-imaginary k-axis (E < 0). Wavefunction is normalisable; δ jumps by π as E rises through threshold.

k = +iκ,   E = −κ²
Resonance

Pole of S in the lower-half k-plane (E = E₀ − iΓ/2). δ rotates by π as E sweeps across E₀ — Slide 3 and Slide 6 show this directly.

E = E₀ − iΓ/2
Virtual state

Pole on negative-imaginary k-axis. Not normalisable, but dominates low-E scattering via a huge negative scattering length.

k = −iκ
Scattering length

Low-E limit: k cot δ₀ = −1/a + (1/2) r₀ k² + … Divergent a when a bound / virtual state sits at threshold (Slide 5).

a = −limk→0 δ₀/k
§ 6 · S-matrix synthesis
The point of this slide: every animation we saw is a projection of the same S(E). Resonances are poles; bound states are poles; virtual states are poles; scattering length is the derivative at threshold. Once students see the animations, this becomes concrete instead of formal.
